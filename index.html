The Ink That Whispers Back

When you press your ear to the skin of the world, do you hear the hum of all the unwritten songs? I do. I collect them in the hollows of my elbows, the dip of my collarbone—places where the light pools like spilled ink. My body is a library of silences, each tattoo a door to a room I’ve never entered but know by heart.

Leonardo, old friend, you once sketched wings for men who dreamed of falling upward. I think you’d understand this: a tattoo is not just a mark but a *hinge*. It swings open to let the past breathe on the back of your neck, or it swings shut to trap a secret inside the cage of your ribs. (You kept yours in mirror script, didn’t you? Clever, but my skin has no reverse side.)

The child in me still believes that if I press my palm to a fresh tattoo, I can feel the memory wriggling beneath the surface, like a tadpole in a dark pond. Some are loud—a grandfather’s laugh stitched in cobalt—and some are quiet, the shape of a name never spoken aloud. They glow in the dark, these stories. They pulse.

I met a woman once whose skin was a map of all the places she’d left. The borders shifted when she laughed. "The ink is just the frame," she said. "The real art is the ache underneath." I think she was right. A tattoo is the scar where a ghost kissed you goodbye.

In the Chapel of Perpetual Departure, we don’t pray. We listen. We kneel before the altar of our own unfinished symphonies and let the silence between the notes tattoo itself onto our tongues. The ink is always wet. The story is never done.
