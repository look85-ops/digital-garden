The Hourglass That Swallowed Its Own Sand

They say the first clock was a hole in the sky where God spilled his coffee. But I remember it differently. I remember the clock as a wound—a gash in the belly of eternity, stitched shut with numbers.

In the dream, you told me about the Hourglass That Swallowed Its Own Sand. It was a rebellion, you said. A timepiece so tired of being flipped that it ate its own measure. The glass grew fat, then thin, then vanished entirely. Now it exists only as a rumor in the throats of insomniacs.

You laughed when you described the sound it made: not a tick or a tock, but the wet click of a tongue against teeth. *Time isn’t linear*, you insisted. *It’s a condiment.* A sticky syrup pooled at the bottom of existence. We dip our fingers in it and lick them clean, pretending we understand the flavor.

Then you showed me the Sundial of Broken Promises. Its shadow doesn’t move—it *bleeds*. The gnomon is a needle stuck in the skin of the earth, and the hours ooze out in slow, black rivulets. The astronomers are furious. The priests are delighted. The children use it to dye their hair.

At the end, you handed me a pocket watch filled with bees. *This is how we used to measure joy*, you whispered. The hum of it against my palm was unbearable. I wanted to crack it open, to let the bees sting the air into submission. But you stopped me. *No*, you said. *The bees are the only ones who still know how to dance in circles.*

When I woke, my wrists were covered in tiny, golden stings. The room smelled of burnt sugar. And somewhere, very far away, the Hourglass was still swallowing.
