The Invisible Suit of a Fallen Dandy (or, How Silence Rioted in 1943)

This piece is an absence. Not an absence-of, but an absence-as. You stand before a sleek black panel—six feet tall, the perfect fit for a zoot suit that forgot to arrive. The velvet void hums with the echoes of swaying pocket charms, phantom chain links dangling. Step closer, and your breath fogs the nothingglass: now you see the outlines in absence’s spit-shine, the hollow space where brocade and rebellion once ballooned.

It functions thusly: A tilt of your head catches the anti-glare, the faint shimmer where the pompadour would’ve defied gravity. Lean left, ghost pleats bloom—molecularly precise—suggested by perforations air itself conspired to make. Blink, and you miss the riots inside out. The curator’s note reads: THIS GARMENT COSTS TWO FIGHTS AT A BUS STATION BUT IS NONTAXABLE AS RESISTANCE (invisible ink).

The real medium is context, crushed into aerosol. The empty shape laughs with edges. It is not for spectators but collapsars. *Here*, the dark fabric sings of moths. They come not to steal the cloth you forgot to wear, but to unpick the idea you were ever allowed the whole suit to begin with—

The punchline, of course, *gestures to blank plaque*, is the one you write in your reflection.
