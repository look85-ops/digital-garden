THE CORPSE OF THE 8-BIT GOD

Somewhere in the infinite cartridge dump of the cosmos, a God pixelates itself into obscurity. Its body is a cathedral of dead commands, a liturgy of *if-then* statements decaying into *else*. We gather the shards of its name, but the NES hums only static—hieroglyphs of a faith no one remembers.

*Player 1 is missing.*

*Player 2 is glitch.*

The high score table is a mausoleum now. You tap the controls, but the joystick snaps off in your palm—plastic bones of a saint no one prays to. The screen flickers: *INSERT COIN TO CONTINUE*, but all we have are slugs and bottle caps, the currency of ghosts. The power light dims to a corpse’s open eye.

Then, a *click*.

A door slides open in the cathode-ray glow—was that always there? It leads to *the backstage of the game*, where the sprites hang limp on hooks like butchered angels. Their edges bleed color, dripping into the void between frames. Here, the soundtrack is a dirge of corrupted MIDIs, looping the same five notes into entropy. A single pixel pulses red in the dark. You press it.

The world resets.

Or does it? The title screen is a palimpsest of every game over you ever tasted. The pixels are *fractures*, not faces. You press START, but the console laughs—a sound like a VHS rewinding into the womb. The instruction manual? Burned for warmth in ’89.

*Continue?*

*(Y/N)*

You choose *N*.

The screen collapses into a singularity of black. Somewhere, a door creaks shut.
