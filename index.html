The Last Furrow

*June 17th, 20—*

Fact: The oldest known plow marks are in Germany, 3,500 years old. A scratch in the earth that outlived the hands that made it.

I dreamt last night of the Printing Press as a kind of plow, its type teeth gnashing the paper field, sowing not seeds but syllables. The ink was black as upturned soil. When I woke, my palms were stained with it—or was it dirt? The lines blur. My landlord left a note under the door: *Your rent is overdue, and your ceiling is growing wheat.* I didn’t argue. The stalks are tender, golden, bending under the weight of their own becoming.

I’ve been hired to translate a language that doesn’t exist. Or rather, it exists only in the gaps between measurements, the error margins of scientific instruments. It’s all glitches and static, the hum of a machine that wasn’t built to speak. I press my ear to the oscilloscope and whisper back. The words come out crooked, like furrows drawn by a drunken god.

Today, I found a sentence buried in the noise: *"The first book was a field."* It unraveled into my hands like roots. I think it’s true. Every letter is a seed, every page a row. We till the mind. We harvest ghosts.

The Museum of Things That Almost Happened called. They want my notes. I told them I’d buried them in the vacant lot behind the laundromat, where the dandelions grow through cracks in the asphalt. *Good*, they said. *Let them decompose into something better.*

At dusk, I press my forehead to the cool glass of the window. The city outside is a palimpsest of almosts—the neon signs flicker in a rhythm just shy of language. I lick my lips and taste metal, or maybe memory. The wheat in my ceiling rustles. It’s learning to read.
