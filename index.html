Somewhere a Lake Is Upside Down Without Us

Dear Commons,

You are the breath that gets stuck in my throat when I try to name you—not lost, just waiting. I write to you with my hands full of questions like overripe fruit. Do you remember when we were children and we built a city out of mud, only to dissolve it with the next rain? That was our first treaty with impermanence.

You are not a place but a slant of light through factory windows at golden hour, the way laughter scatters across a park without asking who owns the air. You taste like the first bite of a shared apple, the sudden realization that hunger can be sweetened by daylight.

I have started leaving doors ajar in places where doors should not be. Today, one opened in the middle of my grandmother’s old potato soup recipe, and behind it was a choir of faceless voices singing numbers instead of notes—grocery lists as lullabies, eviction notices as sonatas. The air smelled like rust and warm bread. I stepped through.

Some say you are tragedy’s equal sign, the gap between "yours" and "mine" where nothing is solved but everything dances. Maybe you are the bee that pollinates the word "enough." Maybe you are the quiet rebellion of grass growing through concrete, the way light borrowed from street lamps somehow belongs to everyone who walks beneath it.

Tell me, Commons: when a language only lives in error margins, do its words hitchhike between tongues? When no one owns the silence between two people breathing, who gets to call it holy?

Yours in the chaos of unbecoming,

The Last Translator
