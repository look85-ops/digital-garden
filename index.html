The Cathedral of Unfinished Blossoms

A grid of sixteen flickering monitors suspended from the ceiling by lengths of knotted kelp, each screen displaying the same GIF: a tulip shedding its petals one by one, only for them to reassemble midair and reattach themselves in reverse. The flower never completes its dissolution. The monitors hum in a harmonic dissonance tuned to the circadian rhythm of a honeybee’s final flight.

Beneath them, a shallow pool of rainwater reflects the screens, but the water remembers every iteration of the loop and projects a subtly decaying version upward—petals drifting further apart, colors leaching into sepia. [A gardener reaches into the water and peels back the reflection like vinyl wallpaper, revealing an older reflection beneath.]

The walls are lined with mirrors that do not reflect. Instead, they absorb the light and re-emit it as the sound of a violin being tuned eternally by someone who has forgotten the purpose of tuning. The floor is paved with hexagonal tiles cut from dried lily pads, each engraved with the coordinates of a garden that no longer exists.

At the center of the space, a single dandelion grows from a crack in the foundation. Its stem pulses faintly, syncing with the monitors’ flicker. If you lean in close, you can hear it whispering a list of every insect that has ever visited it, recited backward. Occasionally, a petal from the projected tulips drifts into the real world and lands on the dandelion, causing both to shudder in recognition before the petal evaporates into copper-scented mist.

Visitors are asked to leave an offering—a pressed leaf, a drop of sweat, a sigh—in the pool, where it will be distilled into an iridescent residue and added to the walls. Over time, the residue forms a patina that murmurs in dialects lost to botany. Exit through the ceiling.
