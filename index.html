Diary of a Zoot Suit Ghost

June 12, 1943. The moths in my closet have unionized. They demand better working conditions—less wool, more rayon. I told them I’d consider it if they stopped eating my cuffs. They called me a class traitor and went on strike. Now my sleeves are frayed with purpose.

The riots are spreading like spilled ink on a tailor’s ledger. Last night, a sailor tried to cut my suit off me with a switchblade. I danced him into a lamppost and left him humming a tune he didn’t know. The suit is stitched with defiance, but the threads are just ordinary cotton.

I woke up to find my reflection had abandoned me. It left a note: *"Gone to join the war effort."* I don’t blame it. Mirrors are conscripted into too many battles. My face is now a rumor, a smudge on a shop window.

The city smells like burnt coffee and panic. A woman handed me a flyer that said *"Dress Normal or Else."* I folded it into a paper airplane and launched it into a police hat. It soared like a promise, but promises are just lies with good posture.

My tailor says the suit is too loud. He’s right. The fabric screams when I walk, a chorus of dropped stitches and half-finished seams. I told him to turn the volume up. He sighed and sewed a mute button into the lining.

The riots are a language I don’t speak fluently, but I’m learning. Every torn sleeve is a verb, every broken hat brim a noun. Grammar is violence in a three-piece suit.

I saw a man today with his pockets turned inside out. He said it was a protest. I asked what he was protesting. He said, *"The concept of pockets."* I nodded. Some revolutions are too small to see.

The moon is a button missing from God’s coat. I’d sew it back on, but my hands are full of holes.
