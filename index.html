The Ceremony of Unstrung Harps

We gather in the inverted ballroom where chandeliers dig their roots into the ceiling, their crystals budding like reluctant orchids. The guests arrive with pockets full of holes, offering their absence as collateral. A man in a moth-eaten tuxedo bows to the empty air, his hands cupped around nothing—*the most generous gift of all*, we murmur, though no one hears it.

The rules are simple: you must give away what you never had. A woman peels her shadow from the floor and folds it into origami swans, releasing them into the chandelier’s branches. They perch there, black and weightless, until the light forgets to cast them. A child offers the echo of a laugh they swallowed at birth. It hangs in the room like a struck bell that refuses to sound.

I present my masterpiece: a symphony of silences, each rest meticulously composed between notes that do not exist. The audience leans in, straining to hear the gaps. Someone weeps at the beauty of it. Or maybe they’re yawning. The difference is a matter of acoustics.

The lie? There is no guest list. We are all crashing.

At midnight, the chandeliers bloom downward, their roots piercing the floor. We applaud the collapse, the splinters, the way the dust settles into a new alphabet. Someone tries to sweep it up, but the broom only stirs the silence into smaller silences. A dignitary from a country that dissolved last Tuesday awards me a medal made of melted keys. I wear it on my tongue.

The potlatch ends when there’s nothing left to give. We leave with our hands full of air, our pockets heavier than when we arrived.
