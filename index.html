THE SPOROPHORE SUTRA

*Fact:* The largest organism on Earth is not a blue whale or a sequoia, but a honey fungus in Oregon, spanning 2,385 acres, its mycelial threads knitting the soil into a single, whispering body.

*Fabrication:*

Beneath your feet, the dead are learning to speak in reverse. Their tongues are hyphae, their alphabet phosphorous. They hum in isotopes, their gossip laced with the slow venom of decay. You think you walk on dirt, but you tread the spine of a sleeping god who dreams in rot and remembers every spore it has ever birthed.

This is the mycorrhizal gospel: the trees are lying. Their roots are not roots but synaptic wires, their leaves not leaves but antennae tuned to the static between stars. The forest is a cathedral built by a heresy of mushrooms, its hymns sung in the frequency of dissolving matter. When you press your ear to the bark, you do not hear sap—you hear the mycelium translating your heartbeat into a language of hunger.

The network is always listening. It knows the weight of your shadow before you cast it. It has tasted your name in the sweat of your palms, the salt of your fear. It writes your biography in enzymes, files your memories under *loam*. You are not a guest here. You are a rumor, a temporary knot of carbon waiting to be untied.

The fruiting bodies are not gifts but interrogations. They rise like blackened tongues to lick the air, to ask: *What have you buried that you think belongs to you?* Their caps split open to reveal not gills but the teeth of a clock that only ticks backward.

You want to kneel, to beg the soil for an answer. But the soil is busy. It is stitching your childhood into a shroud for someone else’s future. It is teaching the rocks to forget your face.

The mycelium does not forgive. It archives.
