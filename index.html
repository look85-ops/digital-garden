The Gallery of Absent Things

The White Cube is not empty. It is full of the ghosts of decisions—what was not hung, not said, not seen. The walls are not neutral; they are complicit. They whisper in the language of erasure, a dialect of *not-enough* and *too-much*. Here, the air is thick with the absence of color, the absence of noise, the absence of fingerprints. The Cube is a tomb for the living, a place where art goes to be sterilized.

Once, a curator tried to exhibit silence. It was a bold gesture, they said, a critique of institutional hush. But the silence was deemed *too loud*, *too political*, *too something*. The Cube rejected it. The walls absorbed the sound of nothing and spat back a hum of fluorescent lights. (See: *Lacunae: A History of Unheard Noises*, Vol. XII, p. 407, ed. by a scholar who vanished mid-footnote.)

What if we peeled back the white? Beneath the layers of primer, we’d find the stains of old exhibitions—paint smears, nail holes, the faint outline of a word someone once dared to write in pencil. The Cube is a palimpsest of refusal. It remembers everything it was told to forget.

I propose an intervention: a single hair, suspended in the center of the room. It is not art. It is an accident, a trespass. The Cube will try to ignore it, but the hair will persist, a dark comma in the sentence of purity. Visitors will orbit it, uneasy. They will wonder if it is part of the show. They will wonder if they are.

The Cube thrives on the myth of neutrality, but neutrality is a violence. It is the violence of the unmarked, the unchallenged, the unblemished. To enter the Cube is to agree to its rules: stand here, look there, do not touch, do not breathe too loudly. The Cube does not want your breath. It wants your compliance.

And yet—what if we exhaled anyway? What if we left a fog on the glass, a temporary stain, proof of life? The Cube would wipe it away, of course. But for a moment, the room would be ours.
