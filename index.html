*The Suture of Laughter in the Gap Where a God Used to Sit

A row of ceramic teeth, too large to belong to anyone living, glazed in the exact color of an egg laid the same morning. Each enamel monument rests, upright, atop a cushion of rust-shed — metal wool grown tender in rainwater or repentance. Together, they form not a mouth, but a horizon-line; molars like rooftops, incisors like watchtowers.

Beside them, a wooden funnel. Its widest aperture faces you. Instructions pinned underneath (calligraphed onto wasp-paper, one sheet a year for thirty years): **Place your voice where it barely fits.**

A pause. The sound inside the funnel is warm and arterial—not quite a whisper, more the shadow-of-waiting just before a tongue presses to a lover’s collarbone in apology. You hesitate (good), then speak. **The sentence no archive will keep for you.** You choose poorly (better). The funnel chews it with old, elegant hands as soft as dusklight between prison bars; a sound-kneading, though only one word re-emerges from the spout:

Unspelled.

Suddenly: seven seconds ago, upstairs, a door that wasn’t there has opened into its frame. Footsteps. (Not yours anymore.) The creak is *surely-linen-woven-between-dog*-teeth. A child (from the past? from the room?) whispers: **You can borrow what falls up.**

In response,—some air exhumes from the wrong side—the last phrase **ever misprinted in a cookbook for the ritual preparation of clocks*******blooms into your hair

**like snowfall remembering rain.**

Everything you still adore moves two millimeters away.

No one is laughing.

**It works**.
