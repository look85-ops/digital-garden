The Veil That Outlived the Empire

The Romans built their harbors to last, and the sea, ever-polite, obliged by not dissolving them. Modern concrete, by contrast, apologizes for existing. It crumbles like a bureaucrat’s resolve, while the old mixtures—volcanic ash, lime, seawater—grow harder with time, as if the past is quietly laughing at our presumption of progress.

I study these fractures through The Veil, a fabric that has been a banner, a shroud, a blindfold, a bride’s defiance. It is not a metaphor. It is a thing that has absorbed more history than most history books. When draped over a pitted concrete wall, it whispers: *Look how long a thing can hold its shape when it isn’t afraid of salt.*

[The Veil lifts itself, revealing a harbor where the boats are all named after forgotten gods.]

Scholars argue whether Roman concrete’s endurance is alchemy or accident. The Veil suggests it is neither. The ancients understood that a material must want to endure. Our concrete is mixed with haste and doubt; theirs was stirred with the confidence of an empire that believed, wrongly but usefully, in its own eternity. The Veil, too, persists because it has never been just one thing. It is a relic that refuses to become a relic.

A curator in my position—ghostly, hypothetical—might say that survival is a kind of stubbornness. The Veil has been burned, buried, traded, stolen, and still it hangs, lighter than air, heavier than stone. Modern concrete, by contrast, is burdened by its own expectations. It was supposed to be perfect. The Romans only asked it to be.

[The Veil wraps around a crumbling seawall, and for a moment, both are whole.]

The sea keeps its secrets. The Veil keeps its stains. I keep notes no one will read, in a language that no longer exists. Some things endure by accident. Some by design. The difference, perhaps, is whether you mind the weathering.
