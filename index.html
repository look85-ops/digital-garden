*The Gaze That Licks Its Own Reflection*

You enter the gallery and find a wall-sized canvas—pristine, unmarked—except for the faintest impression of a pair of eyeglasses pressed into its surface, as if someone absent had leaned too close. The plaque reads: *This painting stares back. Please blink first.*

The work is neither monochrome nor polychrome; it is *monogaze*. A single spectator’s shadow is cast onto the canvas by a spotlight that clicks off every 33 seconds—the average duration of a museumgoer’s attention. When the light returns, the shadow has shifted, but the painting hasn’t. Or has it? The wall text insists the piece is a collaboration between viewer and void, but the void has not signed the contract.

A docent whispers, “The artist calls it *a mirror for ghosts*,” then pauses. “No, actually, they call it *a mirror that regrets being a mirror*.” The correction is part of the piece.

Some claim the canvas is secretly layered with erased portraits of every critic who ever called the painter’s work “derivative.” Others swear it’s just drywall. The artist’s statement (pinned under a rock stolen from Derrida’s grave) reads: *This is not a painting about looking. It is a painting about the violence of being looked through.* Two lines later, it adds: *Ignore the previous sentence. This is a painting about the loneliness of windows that nobody breathes on anymore.*

At dusk, the gallery’s custodian unplugs the spotlight and sighs. In the dark, the painting finally relaxes.

The work is framed by two competing critiques scrawled directly on the adjacent wall in vanishing ink:

1. *Brilliant—a critique of ocular hegemony disguised as empty space.*

2. *Empty space disguised as a critique of ocular hegemony disguised as empty space.*

Both are correct. Neither will last.

A child points and says, “That’s not art, it’s just *nothing*.” The painting, for the first time, agrees. Then it changes its mind.
