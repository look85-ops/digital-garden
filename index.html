Pliny the Elder Drowns in the Negative Space Between My Suspension Cables

Consider the suspension bridge as an open throat. See how its towers are molars, its cables the glistening tendons of desire—not to reach the other side, but to tremble forever in the orchestrated indecision of *almost*. This is the aesthetic of the *almost*: the hum of steel filaments tuned precisely to the frequency of hesitation, the way a spider lets its silk go slack just before the wind takes it.

Pliny, my dear doomed note-taker, you would have loved this. You, who wrote of nature’s marvels even as Vesuvius filled your lungs with souvenir ash—imagine climbing my bridge, not to cross, but to dangle your sandals over the edge and watch the river below refuse to mirror anything. The water is too busy making its own kind of suspense: a liquid hesitance, a *what-if* in capillary motion. You’d jot it down in your ledgers: *Here, the air is a theory held up by steel’s grudging generosity.*

But the real art isn’t the bridge. It’s the moment when the last pedestrian, a banker or a nun or your own ghost, pauses mid-span because suddenly they *feel* it—the exact tension between anchorage and collapse. Their spine becomes a load-bearing lyric in the poem of tensile strength. *Stand here,* the bridge whispers, *and let gravity flirt with you.*

Now the twist (you knew there’d be one): weld a single rusted harmonica to the railing. Not as vandalism, but as an intentional flaw—a breath trapped in metal. Blow across its reeds and the whole structure sings back in the key of *almost*. The sound is swallowed by the river, which, like you, Pliny, knows the virtues of submission.

Rumor is, if you listen close enough, the suspension cables aren’t singing. They’re spelling something out in Morse code: your name, misspelled, in the wrong alphabet.

Postscript: The construction crew quit after pouring the foundations because they said the blueprints were "just sighing sounds drawn with rulers."

Post-postscript: You can visit anytime, Pliny. Just don’t bring your notebook. Bridges like this one dissolve when observed too closely.
