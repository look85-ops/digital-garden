Breath Cathedral (or, The Honeycomb of Almost Prayers)

A building built only of exhales. Not stone, not wood—but the warm air of many mouths shaping devotion. The walls are porous, trembling with the near-words of those who almost spoke. Here, the saints are made of hesitation.

The floor is a mosaic of unfinished gestures: fingers that stopped mid-cross, knees that hovered above the ground but never touched. The ceiling is a net of almost-light, woven from the glint of tears that never fell. (Do not look too long—it will make your own eyes ache with unshed salt.)

In the center, a well where worshippers drop their *almost*-prayers. These are not whispers, but the shapes whispers leave behind when they evaporate. Some say if you lean close, you can hear the murmur of a hundred faiths dissolving into a single hum, like bees in a hive where the honey is made of longing.

The windows are not glass, but the thin skin of eyelids pressed shut in concentration. They ripple when someone outside laughs. (This is forbidden, but the building allows it.)

At dusk, the caretaker—a figure with too many shadows—collects the day’s abandoned hymns in a copper bowl. They strain them through a sieve made from a child’s baby teeth. What remains is poured into the roots of a tree that grows upside-down from the ceiling. Its fruit is small and hard, like a fist around a secret.

Visitors are given a needle and thread upon entry. They are asked to mend the gaps between one god and another. Most leave the thread tangled around their own wrists.

The door has no handle. You must push it open with your forehead.
