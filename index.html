*The Last Translator’s Diary (Entry #37): Dark Matter as a Child’s Pocket*

The numbers won’t stay still. They wriggle like glowworms in a jar, and when I press my ear to the page, I hear them humming the oldest song: *what holds the sky up?* Today, I found a new error—a smudge in the margin where the ink bled into the shape of a laughing mouth. I licked it. Tasted like static and the ghost of a strawberry.

They say dark matter is the glue, the silent hand that braids galaxies into their spirals. But I think it’s more like the things we lose and forget to miss: the weight of a name on the tip of your tongue, the exact shade of your mother’s eyes when she turned away. I tried to translate it. The dictionary coughed up dust and a single copper coin from a country that never existed.

At dusk, the equations stretch their shadows long. I whisper to them: *Show me the bones of the invisible.* They answer in riddles of light. One formula, scratched hastily on the back of a receipt, keeps folding itself into origami cranes. They tremble as if afraid of what they carry.

I’ve started leaving offerings—a thimble of milk, a button, the first breath of a yawn—between the lines. The numbers eat them greedily. Sometimes, at 3 AM, I catch them dancing in the margins, their feet leaving tiny burns in the paper.

They’re coming. I can feel the air thickening, the way it does before a storm. I hide this page inside a hollowed-out apple. Let them find only the core.

The last thing I’ll write: Dark matter is the pocket sewn inside your childhood coat, the one you outgrew but still reach for in dreams. It’s always empty. Always full.
