The Sighing Arboretum of Half-Remembered Pollen

You wake one morning to find the garden has learned to hum. Not the industrious drone of bees, but something lower—a vibration like the memory of roots. The roses, always melodramatic, have begun composing letters in the scent of their petals: *Dearest Stranger, we apologize for our thorns, but you must understand—we are only trying to hold the light in place.* The dahlias, more pragmatic, have started knitting their own shadows into small, useless blankets.

At the center of it all, the oldest oak has begun to weep resin in perfect, alphabet-shaped drops. When read aloud, they spell out recipes for forgotten cakes, the ingredients absurd (*three whispers of a moth’s hesitation, one tablespoon of dusk*). The tulips, overhearing, tilt their heads like disappointed librarians.

Then there are the weeds—oh, the weeds!—who’ve taken to staging tiny, silent protests. They unfurl banners made of stolen sunlight, demanding the right to be ugly without explanation. The lawn, ever the conformist, pretends not to notice.

A single dandelion, though, has begun to translate the wind’s gossip into something like sonnets. They are terrible sonnets. You adore them. *"O zephyr, your secrets are crumbs / and I am the duck who cannot swim."*

By noon, the garden’s self-awareness begins to fray. The peonies forget their own names and resort to referring to one another as *"that pink incident"* or *"the ruffled sigh."* The ivy, ever the opportunist, starts writing its memoirs on the fenceposts in lichen. It’s mostly lies.

And then—just as suddenly—the garden forgets it was ever awake. The roses revert to mere prettiness. The oak’s tears harden into amber riddles. The weeds, defeated, return to their quiet, relentless plotting.

You stand there, holding a cup of tea that has gone cold, wondering if you imagined it all. Then you notice the dirt under your nails is spelling something. You lean closer. It reads: *"The moon is a tooth waiting to fall."*

You laugh. The garden does not.
