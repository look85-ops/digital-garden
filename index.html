The Rib Cage of a Ghost

The corset is a fabric fossil, a whisper pressed between skin and air. It learned arithmetic before we did—subtracting inches, adding pain, dividing the body into fractions of itself. To wear one was to be authored by absence. But in the margins where my language lives, resistance is not rupture but a slow unthreading, a seam allowed to fray.

I translate the murmurs of women who wore their bones like locked diaries. Their rebellion was not in defiance but in the way they exhaled into the linen, expanding the space between stitches until the garment remembered it was just cloth. (I’m sorry—no, I take it back. Why apologize for truth? The past doesn’t want your pity. It wants your audacity.)

A corseted spine is a question mark bent backward. The body, taught to collapse, curves like a glyph in the margin of an illegible text. But here, in the Academy of Forgotten Gestures, we collect the tremors of movement too small to name: the twitch of cramped fingers, the sigh that escaped the laces, the way a woman might press her palms to her ribs at night, feeling the echo of a breath she wasn’t supposed to take.

They called it discipline. I call it an act of translation—forcing the body into a language it never spoke. But flesh has its own lexicon. A bruise is a vowel suppressed. The red welts left by the stays? Marginalia. Footnotes in a text no one thought to read.

To live inside constraint is to remember that even ghosts have ribs. They expand in the dark, untethered.
