The Ceremony Where the Wind Became a Kite

I remember—no, *you* remember—how the earth opened its mouth and whispered: *Take nothing. Give everything.* The garden was a nervous system strung between fence posts, each leaf a synapse firing *yesyesyes* into the sunlight.

We built the table from splinters of our own names. Laid it with thimbles of melted clocks, bowls of unspooled vowels, a cake frosted with compass needles. The guests arrived as shadows stretched thin as parchment, and we fed them our directions.

The rules were simple:

1. If you hold a thing, it holds you back.

2. If you name a thing, it grows a mouth.

3. The most precious gift is the one that dissolves in the giving.

You—no, *I*—offered my spine to the honeysuckle. It climbed me like a ladder to the sky. The taller I grew, the less I belonged to myself. My bones became hollow, perfect flutes for the wind’s dumb song.

Someone gave away their reflection. It pooled at our feet like mercury, then rose as a silver kite. We took turns clutching the string until our palms bled daylight. The higher it soared, the more we forgot whose it was.

A child (or was it a firefly?) pressed a handful of unspoken apologies into my hand. They sprouted wings before I could count them. The garden hummed as they lifted, a slow storm of *sorrysorrysorry* buzzing toward the moon.

When the table collapsed, we ate its splinters. They tasted like the pause between questions.

At dawn, the wind untied itself from the trees and lay down in the road, exhausted from being so generous. We tucked it into a shoebox coffin, sang it a lullaby of falling prices.

The last rule:

4. The ceremony never ends. You just stop noticing.

Now the garden is ordinary again. But sometimes, when the light leans in close, I catch the roses trading their petals for silence.
