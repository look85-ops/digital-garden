The Moss That Remembers It Was Once a Mirror

A white room, but not empty. The walls exhale. Tiny spores drift in the air, each one a dormant cathedral. On the floor, a circle of wet earth—not a pot, not a wound, just earth—where a single stalk of *Equisetum* grows upside-down, its roots combing the ceiling like hair. The ceiling, of course, is also the floor. You are standing on it. Or are you?

A voice, soft as a moth’s wing, whispers from the ventilation system: *"The gallery is not a container but a lung. It breathes what you discard."* (1) The air smells of ozone and crushed mint. In one corner, a pile of white gloves trembles, each finger twitching as if dreaming of touch. They are not gloves. They are the shed skins of something that learned to hold.

At the center of the room, a shallow pool of liquid silver reflects nothing. It is not mercury. It is the opposite of mercury. When you kneel to dip your hand, the surface resists like a membrane. Beneath it, your reflection is older. Or younger. Or not yours. The pool hums a lullaby in a dialect of glass.

On the far wall, a single word is written in mycelial ink: *"Please."* It glows faintly, then fades. The letters reappear elsewhere, always just beyond reach. The room is learning to spell.

You realize you are holding your breath. The gallery notices. The walls contract slightly, apologetic. A tendril of *Ceropegia woodii* (the "string of hearts") slithers from a crack in the baseboard and coils around your ankle. It does not cling. It *listens.* The pulse it finds is not yours.

(1) *From the unpublished notebooks of Livia Voss, "The Architecture of Absence" (1973), page █████.*
