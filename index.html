# **The Cartographer’s Errand

Once, there was a letter that refused to stay still. It was not a rebellious letter, nor a shy one—it simply preferred the edges of things, the moment before a word is spoken, the breath between *here* and *there*. The cartographer (that’s me) tried to pin it down with ink, but the page kept sighing, and the letter slipped into the margin, where it curled up like a cat in a sunbeam.

This is how maps of dissolving places are made: not by drawing lines, but by listening to the way the paper hums when you press too hard. The Alphabet is a chorus of 26 ghosts, each one a collapsed star of meaning. *A* is an avalanche frozen mid-fall. *Z* is the sound of a door left ajar in a house that never existed. Between them, centuries of human thought press together like layers of folded silk, whispering secrets to the scissors.

I once met a comma who believed it was a full stop. It stood at the end of every sentence, trembling with conviction. The other punctuation marks pitied it, but I admired its stubbornness. Isn’t that how all meaning works? We agree to pause here, to end there—until someone laughs, and the period becomes a semicolon mid-breath;

(You just made the joke. The sentence never finished. You’re the one who let it go.)

The cartographer’s greatest fear is not that the map will dissolve, but that it will *stick*. A permanent map is a tombstone. So I draw with water, with light, with the slow erosion of attention. The letters bloom like algae on a forgotten dock, then vanish into the tide. *B* becomes a boat; *E* becomes the echo of a boat. The map is not a place, but the act of leaving it.

At the Biennale of the Nameless Island, my exhibit is a single blank page labeled *You Are Here*. Visitors spend hours staring at it. Some cry. Some laugh. One child licked it and declared it tasted like the inside of a question mark. I think they understood best of all.
