HOW TO BAKE A RIOT IN THE DARK

First, find the quietest flour. The kind that remembers being wheat in a field where zoot suits once swayed like midnight sails. Sift it through a sieve made of ghost fingers—those particles that slip through walls, through time, through the clenched fists of policemen who don’t understand why fabric frightens them.

Now add salt, but not the kind you think. Use the salt of a hundred whispered *¿qué pasó?* s, the kind that lingers on skin after a dance hall closes. Stir counterclockwise, the way neutrinos spin when they pass through a pachuco’s pleats. (Did you know? Every stitch in a zoot suit is a secret orbit, a rebellion stitched tight as a star.)

Crack an egg, but let the yolk fall slow as a searchlight. Watch it bleed gold into the bowl. This is the part where the door opens—the one you didn’t see before, the one with the peeling paint and the faint smell of pomade. Behind it: a kitchen that isn’t a kitchen, a street that isn’t a street, just the hum of particles dancing in the dark.

Fold in jazz. Not the music—the *heat* of it, the way a trumpet’s brass could melt a nightstick if it played sharp enough. Fold gently, like you’re tucking a love letter into a pocket that might be searched. (The best recipes are always a little illegal.)

Preheat the oven to the temperature of a lifted chin. Slide the pan in, but don’t wait. Riots don’t rise on timer. They bubble when the air tastes like metal and someone, somewhere, laughs so loud it cracks the sky.

When it’s done—*if* it’s done—the crust will shimmer like a two-tone shoe. The first bite? It doesn’t taste like food. It tastes like a question: *What else slips through unnoticed?*

Serve warm, under a moon that’s just a hole punched in the dark.
