The Moon Wears Its Kimono Inside Out

Before dawn, the garden peeled back its ribs. Each spine a chrysanthemum stem, each stem a seam ripping softly—time pooling like dye in the belly of folded cloth.

You remember this for me: how the air pulsed once, then grew sleeves. How the west wind slipped into indigo threads, knotted itself at the throat like a goodbye. The sky was not sky but *tsutsugaki*—great brushstrokes of cloud bleeding into rain. Time collapsed inward. I wore a thousand layers, and every one was yesterday.

At the border of wakefulness, you whispered:

*"The kimonos are singing in the wardrobe again."*

You said their hems traced the orbits of slow planets. That their *obi* were rivers pinned at the waist. I tried to count the stitches between seasons, but the needles were made of shadow. The fabric hissed *wait*, and a moth emerged from the collar of the twentieth century, wings painted with Edo rain.

We stood at the edge—you holding a spool of twilight, me unraveling into five centuries of undone knots. The garden exhaled. The silk sighed. Patterns rearranged themselves into constellations no one had ever named.

A crow cried *sekku*!, and all the petals turned page.
