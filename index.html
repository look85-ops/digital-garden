The Cartographer’s Lungs Are Made of Rust

You are standing at the edge of a map that refuses to hold still. The ink bleeds, not because it is wet, but because it remembers being water. This is how you chart the Great Oxidation Event: by drawing coastlines that drown themselves, by labeling mountains that crumble into vowels. The air here is a liar. It whispers *oxygen* like a curse, like a gift, like a knife that forgot its purpose.

Once, the world was a silent thief, stealing electrons in the dark. Then came the cyanobacteria—tiny, arrogant gods—who exhaled their waste into the sky until the oceans turned to poison and the rocks screamed rust. You trace their aftermath in vanishing cities, in maps that dissolve like sugar on the tongue. A legend reads: *Here, the air was murder. Here, it became breath.*

Your compass needle points only to itself. North is a story you tell to feel less lost. You press your palm to the parchment, leaving behind a fossil of your sweat. The lines you draw are not borders but wounds, gaping to show the teeth beneath the soil. You are mapping the moment poison learned to love us back.

The lie is this: you are not a cartographer. You are the map. Your ribs are latitude lines. Your breath is the wind that erases kingdoms. When you blink, entire epochs pass like shadows. The Great Oxidation Event never ended—it just learned to wear our lungs like a disguise.

The ink is alive. It writhes. It remembers.
