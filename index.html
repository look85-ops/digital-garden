Neutrino Psalm for the Unseen Hand

A gallery space, empty except for a single sheet of translucent vellum suspended from the ceiling by fishing line so fine it disappears in certain light. On the vellum, a poem is inscribed in vanishing ink—each word fading as you read it, leaving only the afterimage of meaning. The text is a translation of neutrino collisions into human longing, a liturgy for particles that never stop moving.

Visitors are given Geiger counters tuned to the wrong frequency. They click anyway—random, arrhythmic, like a heartbeat misaligned with time. The sound is piped into an adjacent room where a lone pianist plays a composition titled *"The Silence Between Seconds."* The sheet music is written in a script that resembles both musical notation and the scars left by particle trails in a cloud chamber.

On the far wall, a projection of the sun flickers—not as light, but as a negative imprint, the absence where neutrinos have passed through. Beneath it, a small plaque reads: *"This is not a metaphor for your loneliness."*

The floor is dusted with powdered graphite. Footsteps leave temporary constellations, erased by the next passerby. A docent whispers to no one in particular: *"The average human body is traversed by 100 trillion neutrinos per second. None of them remember you."*

In the corner, a vintage rotary phone rings once every seventeen minutes. When answered, the caller hangs up. A label on the receiver reads: *"Do not disturb—signal in progress."*

The piece concludes with a live performance: a linguist stands at a podium and attempts to pronounce a word that only exists in the error margins of dead languages. The audience hears static. The linguist hears applause.

*"The moon is a postcard we forgot to send."*
