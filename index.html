The Gilded Guillotine

The bowl was born broken. A hairline fracture, delicate as a diplomat’s lie, ran from rim to base. The potter shrugged, poured molten gold into the wound, and called it art.

Now it sits on the altar of the Chapel of Perpetual Departure, cradling dust and the ghosts of last week’s lilies. The gold gleams like a threat. *Look closer*, it whispers. *The flaw is the point.*

Visitors kneel, press their thumbs to the seam, and come away glittering. They leave fingerprints on the reliquary glass, smudged prayers no one will answer. The curator—a woman with a voice like a theremin—tells them, *This is not a metaphor. It is a bowl. It holds nothing.*

Then, at the exact midpoint of this sacred farce, a man in a raccoon coat barges in, trailing the scent of gasoline and convenience store egg salad. He slams a stapler onto the altar. “Fix it,” he demands. The stapler is pristine.

The curator blinks. The bowl does not.

“Sir,” she says, “this is a place of *intentional* ruin.”

He stares at her, then at the bowl, then at his stapler. “So’s this,” he mutters, and leaves.

The chapel exhales. The gold in the bowl hardens a little more.

Outside, the man lights a cigarette with a match struck against the chapel’s marble steps. The flame trembles, then steadies. He pockets the stapler. Somewhere, a potter throws another vessel into the kiln, already dreaming of the cracks.
