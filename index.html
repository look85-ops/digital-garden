*The Ink That Gnaws the Page*

You are standing in a dim room. The walls exhale the scent of rust and linseed oil. Before you, a machine: *not* a printing press, but something older, something coiled tight in its own paradox. Its gears are carved from the ribs of extinct ruminants. Its inkwell is a hollowed-out hornet’s nest, still humming.

The placard reads:

**"This device prints only what has already been forgotten.

Feed it a memory, and it will return it to you as scripture."**

A stack of vellum lies beside it, each sheet thinner than a moth’s wing. Touch one, and it dissolves into your fingertips, leaving behind a taste of burnt honey. The machine’s operator — a figure in a smock stitched from outdated maps — offers you a quill. Its nib is a shard of obsidian, still warm.

"Write," they whisper, "but know this: the words will climb off the page. They will nest in your throat. They will sing in a voice only the ink recognizes."

You write: *Mother, I remember the weight of your silence.*

The machine shudders. The press descends.

What emerges is not text but a slow, creeping stain — the shape of a mouth, open as if mid-scream, or mid-kiss. It spreads across the vellum, digesting the fibers. By morning, it will have eaten through the table.
