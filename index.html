THE BRIDGE THAT ATE ITS OWN SHADOW

Fact: On November 7, 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge twisted itself apart in a 42 mph wind. Engineers called it "aerodynamic flutter." The poets called it *the dance of the commons*—a structure possessed by the rhythm of invisible hands.

Fabrication: The bridge was never meant to hold cars. It was a communal instrument, a vast harp strung with the tendons of fallen cranes, designed to sing the frequency of surrender. The wind? A conspiracy of pigeons, drafting their manifesto in the updrafts. Their demand: *Give us back the air you taxed.*

The bridge did not collapse. It *released*. The concrete was tired of pretending to be permanent. The rebar wished to be unspooled, to become filament for the spiders stitching new constitutions in the ruins. The cables? They slithered into the water, renamed themselves *eusocial snakes*, and founded a utopia where load-bearing was an outdated metaphor.

We are told infrastructure must be *solid, reliable, obedient*. But what if the most resilient thing is the thing that lets go? The bridge, in its final throes, was not failing—it was *teaching*. It demonstrated the arithmetic of disintegration: how to subtract yourself elegantly from the ledger of the useful.

Now we build anti-bridges. Structures that dissolve at the peak of their perfection. A ferry made of murmurations. A tunnel lined with held breaths. The new civic pride is measured in *vanishings per capita*.

The pigeons approve.
