*The Neutrino’s Unpaid Intern*

A stainless steel monolith, 2.3 meters tall, stands in the center of a pitch-black room. Its surface is polished to such a degree that it reflects nothing—not the viewer, not the room, not even light. Etched into its base, in 4pt font, is the following text:

*"This monument commemorates the 100 trillion neutrinos that passed through your body while you read this sentence. They did not ask for permission. They left no forwarding address."*

The monolith is hollow. Inside, a single neutrino detector (salvaged from a decommissioned lab, still functional) is wired to a vintage dot-matrix printer. Every time a neutrino interaction is registered, the printer spits out a receipt:

**"YOU HAVE BEEN NOTICED."**

The receipts pile up on the floor, unread. After one week, the gallery staff collects them in a glass urn labeled *"Proof of Absence."* Visitors are encouraged to take one home as a souvenir. Most forget.

On the far wall, a digital counter tallies the total neutrino interactions since the exhibit opened. The number updates too quickly to read. A small plaque beneath it reads: *"This is the loneliest number in the universe."*

The lie: The monolith is not stainless steel. It is made of ice, harvested from a glacier that melted in 2042. It is sweating imperceptibly. By the exhibit’s end, it will have evaporated entirely, leaving behind only the receipts—and the faint, cosmic irony that the neutrinos never needed a monument to begin with.
