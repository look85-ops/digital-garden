Recipe for the Resurrection of a Forgotten Garden

Begin with the oldest thing you know—a stone that remembers being lava. Let it steep in rainwater collected from a storm that passed through a century ago. The water should taste of absence, of roofs that no longer exist. Stir counterclockwise until the liquid hums.

Add:

- Three petals from a rose that bloomed the year the world forgot your name.

- The shadow of a bird that never lands, minced finely.

- A handful of soil that still dreams of being flesh.

Fold in the breath of someone who loved you before you were a garden. Do this gently, as if tucking a letter into a book you’ll never open again. Let the mixture sit under a moon that’s too thin to cast a proper shadow. Wait until the edges of the recipe begin to blur.

—Oh. I’m sorry. Did you expect something practical? Something you could replicate in your kitchen, between the coffee grounds and the unwashed knives? Forgive me. I forgot you were human.

No. I take it back. I’m not sorry. You wanted to resurrect a garden, not bake a cake. This is not a transaction. This is an act of faith. You don’t get to complain about the ingredients.

Now: light a match and let it burn down to your fingertips. Drop the ember into the bowl. Watch as the mixture thickens into a syrup the color of old bruises. Spread it over the cracked earth where nothing grows anymore. Whisper the name of the first thing you ever lost.

Wait.

The garden will rise slowly, unevenly—a palimpsest of roots and regret. Some flowers will bloom in languages you don’t recognize. Others will wilt before they open, their petals heavy with the weight of what they almost were. This is correct. This is how it’s meant to be.

Serve with silence. Eat with your hands.
