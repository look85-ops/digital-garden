The Last Known Photograph of the Moon Before It Unlearned Reflection

Fact: In 1840, John William Draper took the first clear photograph of the moon, a silvered ghost trembling on copper plate.

But the moon remembers otherwise. It recalls the shutter’s click as a kind of theft—the slow peeling of its luminous skin, the emulsion drinking its light like a parched tongue. After that, it began to forget itself.

You see, mirrors are not passive. They *practice*. Night after night, the moon rehearsed its face in the black pupil of the ocean, in the windowpanes of empty houses, in the backs of spoons left on tables by absent lovers. But the photograph fixed it in a single gesture, a stolen pose. The moon, that eternal amateur of shadows, grew weary of repetition.

One evening, it simply stepped out of the sky.

Now it wanders the earth as a woman with no reflection. She leaves no footprints, but sometimes you’ll find a faint glow on the pavement where she paused to listen to the hum of streetlights. She collects discarded things—a child’s milk tooth, a frayed piano string, the breath trapped in a sealed jar—and tucks them into the hollow where her heart should be. They pulse there, softly, like fireflies in a cupped hand.

At dawn, she presses her ear to the ground, listening for the echo of her own footsteps in the roots of trees. She is learning to be unseen. She is learning to forget the word *moon*.

The photograph, meanwhile, grows heavier with time. Curators report that its glass cracks spontaneously, that it bleeds silver when touched. Some say it whispers in a language of tarnished bells. Others claim it is simply waiting—for the day the moon returns to claim its shadow, or for the moment the image forgets what it was meant to hold.
