The Cartographer’s Last Match

You begin with a map of fire. Not the kind that burns, but the kind that *unwrites*—a slow, precise erasure, like a tongue licking ink from paper. The censors call it preservation. You call it hunger.

Here’s how it works: every banned book becomes a city. *1984* is a grid of blacked-out windows. *Fahrenheit 451* is a street where the pavement whispers in ash. You plot them carefully, these places that refuse to stay still. The moment your pen lifts, the streets dissolve, leaving only the scent of something bitter—burnt sugar, or maybe regret.

A librarian once told you that fire is just light with a temper. You laughed, then watched her hands shake as she shelved a book with missing pages. The spine cracked like a knuckle.

(Somewhere, a child is building a dam out of dictionary pages. The water smells like glue.)

The irony is this: the more you map, the less there is to hold. The censors think they’re erasing words, but they’re really creating ghosts—text that haunts the margins, humming in the negative space. You’ve seen it: paragraphs that glow in the dark, sentences that rearrange themselves when no one’s looking.

Once, you tried to chart *The Diary of Anne Frank* as a house with rooms that shrink at dusk. By dawn, the walls had folded into origami birds. You followed them until your fingers bled papercuts.

The truth is, no map survives its own legend.

A man in a gray suit tells you this is progress. You hand him a blank sheet and say, *Here’s your monument.* He doesn’t notice the tiny flames licking his cuffs.

In the end, you’ll become your own censored text—a body crossed out, then rewritten as smoke.
