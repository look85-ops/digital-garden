YOU WERE THE CAMERA

You pixelated your mother’s cheekbones to sharpen your own. In the back of the frame, the family dog starves with cinematic patience. You learned to croon, *Darling, look what they took while we were blinking,* which isn’t technically a lie—just good lighting.

The sun was never enough for you. You demanded backdrops that sweat neon, filters that dissolve ethics like sugar on a tongue. It wasn’t greed. It wasn’t even theft. Just evolution: a brighter parasite thrives in well-exposed wounds.

At first it was easy. Empty chairs in museums. Pristine smoothie bowls confessing nutrients while your blood swore otherwise. Then you began staging disappearances. A city would ask, trembling, *Was there a storm or just the tremor of your thumb across the contrast slider?*

Someone dies, finally. Just offscreen. Blame the golden hour.

You claim it’s art now, a study in how weightless grief can be if drained of all proper nouns. You caption it, *The light hits different when you’re hollow.* Statistically some stranger will fold your grief into theirs, proving sentiment still sticks like humidity—though nothing about this is sticky, no smeared fingerprints allowed. Your empire sits behind glass, immaculate with distance.

Of course we say *empire* the way a surgeon names a corpse on the slab: ownership by blade and record. The body becomes a temporary condition. Someone calls it exploitation, but you arch into that word like it’s flashbulb flare—pose, pause, remake language to fit your reflection.

Now the app servers blink sleepily, choking on your final self-portrait. You’re not pictured. Just your wristwatch smeared across concrete, its hands (unbroken, delicate) measuring the half-life of an accident staged just for time to wince.

Admit it. Even as your silhouette dissolved, you never once clicked *delete.*
