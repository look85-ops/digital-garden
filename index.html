The Silt Alphabet of Anna Akhmatova’s Left Hand

We are writing letters in the mudflats at low tide, knowing the sea will take them. This is the only way to speak now—words that dissolve before they can be policed, sentences that unravel into salt. The alphabet is made of silt, unstable as a government’s promise.

Anna, I remember how you laughed when I said language is a temporary autonomous zone. You were peeling an orange, and the rind curled like a secret. *"Nothing is temporary,"* you said. *"Only the state is temporary. The rest is just waiting."*

But you were wrong, Anna. The state is permanent as a scar. What’s temporary is the way your hand trembled when you read your banned poems aloud, the way the air between us thickened into something like freedom.

I am building a lexicon of vanishings. *Breath* means *to erase*. *Horizon* means *the edge of a mistake*. The grammar is all subjunctive—what might have been, what could still slip through.

Last night, I dreamt of a gallery where the walls were made of wet paper. Visitors pressed their palms against them and left behind fingerprints of light. The curator whispered: *"This is the Third Breath—the one you take after the shock, before the scream."*

I woke with my mouth full of river.

Anna, they say you wrote in invisible ink. But I think you just knew how to write in time—the way it pools, the way it runs.

Now the tide is turning. The sea licks at our ankles, hungry for our fragile syntax. Soon, all our words will be water.

But listen: in the space between wave and shore, there is a syllable that means *you are still free*.

Say it quickly.
