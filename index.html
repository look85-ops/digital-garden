The Persistence of Decay

Fact: Johannes Kepler coined the term "Camera Obscura" in his 1604 treatise *Ad Vitellionem Paralipomena*. He was trying to understand why stars twinkle. He failed. Stars, it turns out, are merely photons suffering existential indecision mid-flight. **The Persistence of Decay** begins here, in Kepler’s inkwell, where the spilled night sky pooled and refused to dry.

Imagine the room: womb-dark, smelling of damp plaster and forgotten potatoes. A pinhole bleeds the outside world onto the opposite wall. A cart rattles past upside-down. A pigeon defecates cosmically upon the chimney pots. The image trembles. It *lives*, briefly, this stolen slice of elsewhere. But photons are fickle tourists. They arrive, gawk at the inverted spectacle projected onto the plaster (a smear of ochre, a ghostly hat), then promptly forget why they came. They slump. They pool. They become lukewarm puddles of spent curiosity. The cart dissolves into grainy regret. The pigeon’s offering blurs into a melancholic Rorschach blot.

We built boxes to trap this fugitive light-show. We polished lenses until they wept silver. We called it Art, Science, Truth. But the photons giggled. They knew the secret: the image is always decaying *as* it forms. Like cheese left in a damp cellar. Like memory. Like Kepler’s treatise slowly being eaten by enthusiastic booklice dreaming of starlight.

The Museum of Things That Almost Happened displays a single, fist-sized lump of hardened photons recovered from a collapsed Camera Obscura in Leipzig. Label: *Regretful Afternoon, circa 1621. Contains traces of inverted laundry and existential doubt.* Touch it? It feels like lukewarm static, humming a forgotten lullaby. Nearby, a fossilized afterimage of a hat floats in a jar of distilled twilight. Curators whisper it occasionally whispers back: *"Turn me right-side up. The ceiling is crawling."*

The universe, you see, is merely a vast, poorly maintained Camera Obscura. God is a distracted janitor, mopping up spilled galaxies with a worn-out sponge. Occasionally, He peers through the pinhole – our sky – and wonders why everything looks so *small*, so *upside-down*, and frankly, a bit grubby. He shrugs. Flicks off the sun. The image dissolves.
