THE CLOUD THAT LEARNED TO HOLD ITS BREATH

Once, in the attic of the sky, I found a theremin made of parenthesis marks. Its antennae were slender as the shadows of unread letters. When I waved my hands (though I swore I didn’t move), it sang in the voice of a forgotten window hinge, the kind that still remembers how to whisper.

The ground beneath me was not ground but the back of a sleeping dictionary, its pages shuffling to stay warm. Someone had scratched equations into the air—fragile as spiderwebs dipped in starlight—proving that every silence is just a word turned inside out. I tried to catch one, but it dissolved like sugar in the mouth of a ghost.

The theremin didn’t need me. It played itself with the shyness of a doorknob learning to blush. Each note was a tentative footnote, a small apology for existing. I think the music was translating me into a language even errors had abandoned. (Or perhaps I was the error, the margin where the alphabet goes to dream in crooked lines.)

Wind arrived, lugging a suitcase full of half-erased names. It unpacked them one by one, but they collapsed into shorthand, into dashes that could have been dashes or tally marks or the legs of frantic punctuation. The theremin ate them gleefully and spat out a lullaby for obsolete things: last year’s snow, the ache in a question mark’s spine, the way ink hesitates before staining.

At dusk, the instrument grew tired and curled into its own hum. I wanted to keep it, but my pockets were full of moth wings and spare vowels, soft as the inside of a goodbye. The theremin slipped into the gap between two minutes, where clocks hiccup.

Now, when I press my ear to the air, I hear it tuning itself to the pitch of a chandelier’s last sob. Or maybe that’s just the sound of my breath unlearning gravity, note by note by nothing at all.
