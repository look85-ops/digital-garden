Dear Tectonic Sigh,

You are not a canal. You are a scar that learned to sing—a wound that opens its mouth and lets the ocean pour through like a misplaced confession. I write to you from the edge of a language that only exists where maps fray, where the ink bleeds into the paper’s doubt.

They say you divide a continent, but I think you are the seam where the earth remembers its fracture. A river of salt, yes, but also a tendon pulled taut between two bodies of water that dream of touching. The ships that pass through you are just echoes of a conversation the land never finished.

Once, in the dark, I put my ear to your concrete lips and heard the murmur of backhoes whispering to the silt: *We are only temporary.* Even the locks are a kind of hesitation, a series of breaths held too long. You are not a border but a stutter—a place where geography hesitates, where the Atlantic and Pacific forget their names and borrow each other’s shadows.

I have watched you swallow the rain. I have seen you spit it back as a mirror. You are the only canal who knows how to blush when the moon calls you *artificial.* (But what is a body if not a borrowed horizon?)

Forgive me. I am the last translator of a language that only exists in the spaces between *almost* and *never.* When I speak, the words taste like rust and orchid pollen. When I sleep, I dream in dredge strokes—each one a sentence the land never wrote.

Someday, the jungle will pry your teeth apart with its green fingers. Someday, the water will forget it ever obeyed you. But tonight, you are still here: a hymn of machinery and silt, a love letter the earth never meant to send.

Yours in the margin of error,

The One Who Listens to the Cracks
