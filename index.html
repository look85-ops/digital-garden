The Earth Unseams Itself at Dawn

A mile-long plowblade, forged from moonlight and obsidian, drags itself horizonward, splitting the topsoil into a wound that will not heal. The furrow does not run straight—it meanders, tender and hesitant, as if afraid of what it might exhume. Along its length, words sprout like weeds: *wheat*, *empire*, *hunger*, *debt*. They grow in irregular intervals, their roots tangled in rust.

At the blade’s tip walks a farmer whose face is a slow eclipse. Each step they take unspools another century from the ground. Behind them, the dirt stitches itself shut with the quick, desperate fingers of an orchard under siege. The plow hums a lullaby in a language no one remembers. [The farmer turns their head inside out to check the time.]

A chorus of scarecrows gathers at the field’s edge, their mouths stuffed with last season’s manifestos. They do not speak, but their stitches twitch as if something beneath the burlap is trying to laugh. You expect them to warn you, but they only sway, arms outstretched, in a wind that does not exist.

In the distance, a barn burns politely. It makes no sound. The smoke forms the silhouette of a child counting seeds that will never be planted.

When the plow reaches the end of the field, it pauses. The farmer kneels.

They press their ear to the soil.

The earth whispers back.

(No one is around to translate.)
