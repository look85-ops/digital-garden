THE HOUSE THAT SANG ITSELF TO SLEEP

Listen—the hills are not hills. They are the backs of giants who knelt down one day and forgot to rise. Their skin is grass. Their breath is wind. Their bones are the long, slow songs of roots. We build monuments on their shoulders and call it *landscape*, but really, we are decorating graves.

A child knows this. A child digs a hole and whispers into it, *"Grow me a staircase."* The earth obliges. By morning, the hole has become a tower. By noon, the tower is a mouth. By dusk, the mouth is singing a lullaby in a language made of teeth and honey.

This is how you make art that lasts:

1. **Bury your name in wet clay.** Let the worms rewrite it.

2. **Build a bridge from one shadow to another.** Do not ask where it leads.

3. **Plant a question mark in the soil.** Water it with laughter. (The punchline is—)

The oldest fairy tales are not stories. They are *instructions*. *"Follow the breadcrumbs."* *"Hold your breath past the graveyard."* *"Never look back, but if you do, make sure you’re holding a mirror."*

We are running out of mirrors.

So here is the new rule: When the earth offers you a gift—a stone shaped like a heart, a tree bent into a chair, a river that hums your mother’s name—you must take it with both hands and say, *"Yes, but only if it hurts a little."*

The best art is a door that only opens when you stop knocking.

(You’ve been knocking for years.)
