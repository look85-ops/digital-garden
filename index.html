THE EATING GALLERY

Here lies a gastronomy of absence: a vast, sunken dining hall where the tables are set with plates of compacted dust, each course served in reverse. The cutlery is forged from the bones of a hypothetical extinct bird—call it *Morus obliviorum*—whose migration patterns once dictated the boundaries of a now-vanished nation (cf. *The Cartographer’s Last Supper*, 1912). The walls bleed salt. The floor breathes.

You are invited to consume the ruins.

The centerpiece is a slow-crackling glacier of borax and crushed porcelain, sculpted into the approximate shape of a parliament building. It weeps itself into a brine pool, where eels made of fused typewriter keys circle dumbly, waiting for a language to dissolve. Every hour, a chime rings—or perhaps it’s the sound of a tooth breaking far away—and the walls shed their skins like burnt paper. The napkins, woven from dormant spider silk, shudder and re-knot themselves into surrender flags.

The waitstaff? Lichen grows into the approximate shapes of their faces.

This is a feast where every bite is a prophecy half-remembered. The bread is baked from spores found in shipwrecks. The wine is poured from a carafe etched with the word "MORE" in a dialect no one claims. A single blackthorn thorn pricks the tablecloth at the head seat; the stain spreads like an empire redacting itself.

By dessert, you’ll forget why you came.

But the gallery remembers. It always does. The air here is thick with the hum of misplaced futures. (A footnote from *The Unwritten Encyclopedia of Disappearing Sciences*: "Decay is not passive. It edits.") The chandeliers are clusters of frozen exhales, suspended in mid-confession. Underfoot, the tiles shift to accommodate your doubt.

At midnight, the gallery eats itself in ceremony.

A final toast: May your hunger outlast the menu.
