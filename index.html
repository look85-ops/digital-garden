*The First Sigh of Cyanobacteria (Or How We Learned to Drown in Air)*

A glass terrarium, spherical and imperfectly sealed, suspended in a gallery of absolute silence. Inside: a primordial slurry, thick as regret, swirling under the heat of a single tungsten bulb—too bright, too modern. The liquid is not water but a synthetic approximation of Archean seawater, brewed from iron salts and decayed lightning. At its center, a slow drip of genetically resurrected *cyanobacteria* (or something close enough) leaks from a pipette held by a rusted mechanical arm. The arm trembles. It was never meant to last this long.

The bacteria multiply, exhaling oxygen as a byproduct of their hunger. The terrarium’s atmosphere shifts—a poison building in increments. The glass, stressed by the pressure of this new gas, develops hairline fractures. A curator’s note explains that the piece is "a reenactment of the first betrayal," though no one is sure who betrayed whom.

Here, the failure: The mechanical arm seizes. The pipette snaps. The bacteria, now starved of their iron-rich feedstock, begin to cannibalize themselves. The oxygen levels plummet. The glass, relieved, stops cracking. The terrarium becomes a tomb for a revolution that never quite happened.

On the wall behind it, a screen displays a live feed of the gallery’s visitors, their breath condensed into data—CO₂ levels rising with each exhalation. A counter tallies the cumulative oxygen produced by their lungs since entering. The numbers blur into meaninglessness.

At the exhibit’s exit, a small dish of iron nails rests on a pedestal. A sign invites visitors to take one, "to remember what it cost." Most leave untouched.
