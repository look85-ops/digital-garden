THE TENDERNESS OF GLOWING SHARKS

A gallery wall hums, not with sound, but with its opposite: the static-electric thrill of *listening to light*. The piece is composed of fourteen GIFs—no, not GIFs. *Absences*. Each frame (if one could call a hollow a frame) holds the imprint of a creature that was never born. The first sequence: a shark made of bioluminescent teeth, looping through the same graceful lunge toward a seam of inky water. (You cannot hear its joy, but you can feel it pass through you like X-rays through a lung.) The second sequence: the same shark unspooling, its luminous body resolving into a diagnostic scan—fluorescent wireframes pulsing to the rhythm of an ICU heart monitor. A heartbeat, or maybe just integers counting down to zero. The GIF never crashes. It does not even try.

Then—the third loop. Someone has left a spoon on the edge of the display. A real spoon, cheap and steel. It belongs here in a way you cannot dispute. Slowly, by increments no scientist could measure, the spoon begins *leaning* toward the sharks. By the thirty-seventh viewing, it is poised above the screen like a pendulum. You think it might fall. It never falls. Between the spoon and the sharks, something has been negotiated silently, tenderly, the way plants agree without words about the angle of the sun.

This is how it feels to witness the sharks (are they sharks?)—first as euphoria, then as specimen. Notice how easily your sense of *creature* becomes *data*. How eagerly you believe an algorithm. How much you wish for the spoon to finally slip, to shatter the screen, to free—what?

A question (taped carefully to the adjacent wall):

*Who polished a star into this shape?*
