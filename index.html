The Cartographer of Vanishing Alleles

You ink the coastline of a gene that unravels as the pen lifts. The map is a confession in reverse: your grandfather’s hunger, your mother’s silence, the way your hands shake when you lie. The paper drinks the lines like a throat.

Here, a village of methyl groups huddles under a storm of scissors. There, a bridge of histones collapses mid-stride. You chart the erosion of memory—how trauma etches itself in phosphorus, how the body becomes a palimpsest of unfinished funerals.

A door opens in the spine of the manuscript. You step through.

Inside, a room stitches itself from half-remembered light. The walls are lined with jars of expired epigraphs: *Here lies the gene for joy, dormant since the war. Here, the allele for forgiveness, fossilized in salt.* You pocket one. It dissolves like sugar on the tongue.

Outside, the map is bleeding. The borders waver—your grandmother’s laugh, your father’s absent gaze—all dissolving into the margins. You try to anchor them with punctuation, but the commas curl into question marks. The legend reads: *This is not inheritance. This is theft.*

You press your palm to the paper. It pulses. Somewhere, a chromosome folds into an origami of smoke. Somewhere, a door you didn’t paint swings shut.

The cartographer’s paradox: to draw a place that exists only when you look away.
