The Last Streetlight Before the Void Bows Its Head

I paint with the holes left behind when pigeons take flight—those startled absences that hang in the air like unfinished curses. My latest piece: a sidewalk where a stranger’s shadow once lingered too long, now preserved in negative space. You can walk through it, but it walks through you harder.

Cities are cathedrals of accidental intimacy. We memorize the cadence of a neighbor’s cough through thin walls, the way a barista’s hands tremble at dawn, the precise angle a man leans against a bus stop as if the pole alone holds the universe together. These are our shared rituals, though we never speak of them. My art captures the silence between the ritual and the ruin.

Last Tuesday, I installed an invisible bench in the park. It only exists when someone sits where it isn’t. So far, three people have used it. One cried. Another left a single glove behind. The third was a dog.

(Here is the sentence that belongs elsewhere: *The moon is a broken bicycle wheel spinning in a lake of distilled silence.*)

Critics call my work "aggressively ephemeral." I call it *ghost choreography*. Every city is a hall of mirrors where we keep bumping into reflections of people who’ve already turned the corner. I once tracked a stranger’s coffee stain across nine blocks—a Rorschach blot migrating like a nomadic god. It vanished at the subway stairs. I consider this my magnum opus.

Tonight, I’m etching the outline of a whispered secret onto a fogged-up window. By morning, it’ll be gone. But for one breath, someone’s unspoken words will have trembled in the glass like a vow.

The Gallery of the Third Breath is hosting my retrospective: *Thirty Years of Unseen Things*. The centerpiece is an empty frame titled *The Exact Moment You Realized You’d Forgotten Their Face*. Visitors stand there for hours, waiting to feel something. Most leave disappointed. A few leave lighter.
