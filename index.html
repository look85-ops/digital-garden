The Ceramic Leak

*March 14, 2047*

The Baghdad Battery was a clay jar, a bit of copper, an iron rod—maybe a battery, maybe not. That’s the fact. The rest is voltage.

I dreamt last night of the White Cube Gallery, but it wasn’t white. It was the color of a screen left on too long, a blue so faint it hummed. They’d placed the Battery in the center, lit by a single LED disguised as an oil lamp. The label read: *"Object may contain ghosts."* Visitors circled it like vultures who’d forgotten what meat looked like. One woman touched the glass and whispered, *"I think it’s charging my phone."*

The curator (a ghost, or a very tired intern) explained that the jar was now an artwork titled *The Ceramic Leak*. "It’s about the seepage of time," they said, "or maybe just a bad seal." The irony, of course, is that we don’t know if the original ever held anything. Acid? Wine? A love letter dissolved into slurry? Now it holds our projections, which are heavier.

I asked if they’d tried to power the gallery with it. The ghost laughed—a sound like a loose wire brushing metal—and said, *"We did. The lights dimmed. Then the security system died, and someone stole the exit signs."*

Later, in the gift shop, they sold replica batteries filled with sand. The label promised *"2,000 years of potential energy."* I bought three. When I shook one, it made a sound like a coin dropped in an empty museum.

Back in my studio, I wired them to a dead lightbulb. Nothing. Then I realized: the power was never in the jar. It was in the *gap*—between the rod and the copper, between fact and fiction, between the artifact and the white room that turned it into a god.

I left the setup on my desk. By morning, the bulb was flickering. Or maybe I just imagined it.
