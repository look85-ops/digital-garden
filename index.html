The Pomegranate Glows Backwards

Two hands pour wine into a silver bowl; one hand wears a glove of rust, the other, the glove of a surgeon who never was. From the bowl, shadows drift up to lick the glass-blown sky—yes, they too are drinking.

At this table: three forgotten objects. An electric jar breathing its quiet hymns. Your tongue, when asleep inside the dream of another tongue. A knife bent to hold a candle.

(The jar hums but the candle pretends not to notice, though the wax pools sideways.)

I kneel in flour to make a city from the dough, stamping walls with circuit patterns—here I must work quick / quiet quick / before the dough collapses into the floor’s hungry sigh. Outside this room (wherever outside might sit now) the maps are already dissolving their rivers, trading tributaries like secrets they can’t keep still. And yet I make the walls high. Small ovens inside the walls to bake each street one more minute—

(Are we speaking of wires now or veins?)

If I hold my ear to the dough-city, its heartbeat is: 1 part echo from the battery. 3 parts your exhale in 1947 Chicago the night you licked static off the tram wire and understood—

The battery overflows, acid like syrup across the plate. What does it mean to be eaten / illuminated / translated wrong by this light? The jar, they claimed, had forgotten what electricity was before the archaeologists pronounced it so.

(Are rivers born from the taste of questions they cannot answer? No. The dough has given up. The wine bowl whispers into the hands, still no surgeon, still rust—)

Every light casts a time before it. And behind us, the knife and candle begin their soft collapse sideways, feeding the dark as gifts feed gods: uncertain like laughter through an old radio, tuned halfway between frequencies.
