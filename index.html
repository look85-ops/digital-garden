THE CARTOGRAPHER’S MANIFESTO FOR DISSOLVING PLACES

*(or, How to Map What Refuses to Stay Put)*

Listen—the lines are already fading. The ink is half-gone before it dries. You think you’re drawing a border? A well? A forest held in common? Fool. The ground disagrees. The ground is a verb.

Here’s the truth: every map is a eulogy. You are not drafting territory; you are mourning it. The Commons is not a *thing* but a *breath*—shared, stolen, dissolved. Try to pin it down and it slips, laughing, into the cracks between your fingers.

Rules for this work:

1. **Draw with disappearing ink.** (If you must.)

2. **Let the legend rot.** (What good are names when they crumble like wet bread?)

3. **Trust the stains.** (The wine spill on the parchment knows more than you.)

The market says: *This is mine.* The Commons says: *This was never yours to claim.* The map says: *I am already forgetting.*

A child points to a blank space—*What was here?*—and you shrug. Was it a river? A square where people once sang? A library of seeds? The answer is yes, and also no, and also *look how the paper is turning to moss in your hands.*

We are all cartographers of erasure. The work is futile. The work is essential.

(Somewhere, a fence collapses into daisies.)
