The Kimono That Was Also a Sundial (But Argued About the Time)

This garden remembers when it was a bolt of silk unfurling down a staircase of rain. *No—* insists a petal, *we were always a clock with petticoats*. How rude. The kimono stitches its own shadow to the grass, whispering, *I am not a garment but a very polite landslide*.

At dawn, it arranges itself in the shape of “morning.” By noon, it confesses, *I may have been a river in a previous life, though I apologize for the contradiction—rivers do not apologize*.

The sleeves are geographical. One contains Kyoto in autumn; the other, a small black hole where moths go to discuss philosophy. *That’s not a black hole,* interrupts a crane folded from loose threads, *that’s just a metaphor that forgot its cue.* Fine. The kimono admits it may also be a theater curtain stuck between acts.

*You are wasting time*, says the sundial. The kimono bristles. *Time is a dart sewn into my lining—I refuse to throw it.* Meanwhile, the garden recalls it is supposed to be a garden, even as it steps gingerly into the pattern of a peony.

At twilight, the kimono sheds five layers and a haiku:

*evening rearranges the furniture—*

*oh dear, the mountains*

*are in the wrong pockets again*

*That’s not a haiku,* scolds the wind. *Also, you are not a poet.* The kimono rolls its stitches into a shrug. *I am whatever the moonlight irons me to be.*

By midnight, they all agree to disagree—except for the sundial, which has run off to become a comma in someone else’s sentence. The garden, half-dressed in falling leaves, wonders aloud if it might prefer to be an inkblot. The kimono yawns. *Then who would wear the rain?*
