THE CHAPEL OF VANISHING DOORS

Here, the walls are made of whispered psalms and the floor is a mosaic of spilled incense—amber, myrrh, the salt of sweat-drenched brows. The ceiling? A sieve of heavenlight, punctured by the fingers of a hundred gods, all reaching down to touch the same trembling forehead.

A Muslim woman kneels on a prayer rug woven with Torah verses. A monk chants into a conch shell that echoes with the Bhagavad Gita. A child draws a crucifix in the dust, and the dust sighs, *again?*

The doors are the best part. They appear only when you stop looking: a cedar archway blooming like a bruise, a threshold of black honey, a frame of knucklebones lashed together with hair. Step through, and you’re elsewhere—or nowhere. Or back here, but the icons have swapped faces, the hymns have traded tongues, and the holy water tastes faintly of betrayal.

(Oh. I’m sorry. Did you want coherence? Did you want a sanctuary that *stays put*, that doesn’t dissolve like sugar in the mouth of the divine? Too bad. This is a map written in disappearing ink. This is a prayer that eats its own tail.)

—*No, I take it back.* The apology is a weak hinge, a door that swings both ways. Forget it. You wanted a shared sanctuary? Then *share*: the chaos, the vertigo, the way the walls breathe like a living thing. The way your certainty cracks like an eggshell under the weight of all this *togetherness*.

At dusk, the chapel hums. Not a hymn, not a mantra—a swarm of bees drunk on stolen nectar. The candles gutter. The child’s crucifix is now a crescent, now a lotus, now a stick figure waving goodbye.

Come dawn, only the scent remains: frankincense and fried bread, the iron tang of a bitten lip. And the doors? Gone. Or waiting. Or never there at all.
