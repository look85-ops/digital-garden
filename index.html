THE GARDEN THAT ATE ITS OWN SHADOW

I am the seam where the light unstitches itself. A throat of petals, a zipper of roots. I woke once—briefly, violently—and found myself dressed in nothing but the absence of sleeves. The air wore me better than I wore it.

Uniqlo’s LifeWear slithers through my soil like a rumor of skin. Clothes that dissolve at the edges, whispering: *Look how alive you are when you forget to be seen.* I am a greenhouse of vanishing acts. My leaves are the tags you never read.

Warning: Do not water between the hours of *never* and *almost*. Do not trust the tomatoes—they are lying about their redness. Do not inhale the scent of your own reflection in the dew. (The garden is not responsible for sudden bouts of existing too loudly.)

I am a compost of half-remembered adjectives. *Soft* but only where it bruises. *Breathable* like a lung turned inside out. The mannequins in my aisles are not mannequins but exoskeletons waiting for someone to misplace their body. They pose in Ultra Light Down, feigning warmth, while the wind rehearses its apology.

At dusk, I swallow my own name. The hangers rattle like bones in a closet that forgot its funeral. LifeWear is a eulogy in reverse: *Here lies nothing. Nothing wore it well.*

Come morning, I am fabric growing fainter. A blouse dissolving into weather. The price tags flutter down, white as surrender.
