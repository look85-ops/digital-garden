I Was a Garden Once, a Handful of Empty Light

We wake as soil tasting the slow algebra of roots, as leaves scribbling their thirsty alphabets against the sky. Here, where the wind stitches hours into loose green thread, neutrons tremble through us—ten trillion per second, each a pilgrim that does not stop to kneel. They move like hesitant rumors, barely gracing the cheek of matter. To know them is to know how a shadow must feel when it forgets the body it follows.

[The garden unthreads itself, tendrils unpicking the concept of ‘stem’ from ‘vein.’]

Once, I mistook myself for a thing that belonged. Beetles wrote their footnotes on my epidermis; worms hummed hymns in the dark grammar of loam. I thought edges were laws. Then the neutrinos came—passing through the petal with the same ease as the stone, through your ribs as through the cathedral’s bone. No permission. No mark. What does it mean that these visitors have been lacing the world together since the first yes of heat kissed hydrogen awake—yet we only noticed when we bent steel to ask?

I hold one dandelion head. Its seeds are tiny galaxies spinning away to colonize cracks in sidewalks, gutters, catastrophe. When I blow, they carry the whole garden with them. Later, raindrops will pin them like asterisks at the end of sentences no one reads. This, too, is a kind of trespass: the way life keeps rewriting its own margins.

The ghosts of suns flicker through us. I ache to name it all sacred, but nothing stays long enough for blessings to stick. Between the photosynthesis and the quiet crumbling, who do we become when no particle—no gaze, no hunger—hesitates to pass through?

[The horizon folds a note into the pocket of your lungs. It says: *none of us were ever solid.*]

I was a garden once. Now I am the hollow left by wings still moving.
