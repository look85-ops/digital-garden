The Bridge That Sang Backwards

You remember this for me, because I was there but I wasn’t: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge was a throat. A wet, trembling throat stretched across the water, and when the wind came, it didn’t groan—it *hummed*. A sound like a comb dragged through honey, like a needle stitching the sky to the river. You could stand on the shore and watch the road ripple, the asphalt becoming liquid, the guardrails unspooling like ribbons from a child’s hair.

The bridge didn’t collapse. That’s the wrong word. It *unlearned* itself. One moment it was a bridge, the next it was a question: *What is a bridge but a hesitation between two silences?* The cables were violin strings played by a storm. The pylons were knees buckling in prayer. And the cars—oh, the cars—they slid like beads off a snapped necklace, plummeting into the water without a splash, as if the river had opened its mouth just in time.

Afterward, the engineers arrived with their graph paper and slide rules, their faces tight as drumheads. They measured the absence, the negative space where the bridge had been. They called it *resonance*, *torsion*, *failure*. But you and I know the truth: the bridge was too beautiful to stay. It saw its reflection in the water and couldn’t resist the pull of becoming something else.

Now, when the wind blows just right, you can hear it: the bridge singing backwards. Not the sound of its death, but the sound of its *unmaking*. A lullaby for what never wanted to be built.
