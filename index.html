*The Cartographer’s Ghost Eats Its Own Pixels*

The map is a lie that insists on its own truth, a glitch in the system of remembering. You trace the coastline of a childhood game level—*there*, where the lava flickered like a bad VHS tape, *there*, where the jump button betrayed you—but the pixels dissolve under your cursor, leaving only the afterimage of a cheat code you swear you never used. Nostalgia is a corrupted save file. You press *load*, but the screen fills with static, a chorus of 8-bit ghosts laughing in the key of *you were never here*.

The chiptunes hum: *This is not a memory. This is a taxidermied emotion.* The industry of longing sells you back your own fingerprints, pixelated and sanitized, but the cartridge is cracked. You blow into it anyway, a ritual as futile as praying to a dead god. The game boots. The game crashes. The game was never real.

(But what if it was? What if the pixels *are* the ghosts, and the ghosts are hungry? What if the cartographer’s hand trembles not from fear, but from the weight of a world that only exists when drawn?)

No. The map is a palimpsest written in disappearing ink. You argue with yourself: *The past is a locked door.* Then you disagree: *The past is a door that was never closed, just poorly rendered.* The 16-color palette of memory bleeds into the present, staining it with the wrong shades. You try to correct them, but the undo function is broken.

(And yet—what if the glitches are the truth? What if the skipped frames and garbled audio are the only honest parts, the places where the machine admits it, too, is lost?)

The gallery walls hum with the sound of a thousand CRT screens sighing. The cartographer’s ghost eats its own pixels, a self-cannibalizing act of creation. You watch, but your eyes are made of the same unstable code. The screen flickers. The map redraws itself. The chiptunes stutter.

You are here. You are not here.
