The Last Translator’s Alphabet of Broken Light

Imagine a language that only lives in the spaces where things don’t quite fit—where a teacup’s handle wobbles, where a sentence stutters, where your shadow lags half a second behind you. This is the tongue I speak, the one I mend with gold-dusted silence.

Today, I am repairing the word for *moonlight*. It shattered when a child tried to catch it in a jar. Now, its fragments glow in my palms like shy fireflies. I solder them with strands of my own hair (which, according to the *Atlas of Unwritten Constellations*, is the only adhesive that remembers laughter). The cracks become rivers. The rivers become bridges. The bridges hum lullabies in a key only broken things can hear.

Some words are too damaged to translate. The word for *goodbye*, for instance, is now a mosaic of breath and static, assembled from the pauses between radio signals. I wear it as a pendant. It weighs nothing and everything.

A critic once asked me, *Why preserve what was never whole?* I handed them a spoon bent by a century of stirring. They understood. (As noted in *The Encyclopedia of Absent Answers*: *"A curve holds more questions than a straight line."*)

The final word in my dictionary is *______*. It looks like an empty shelf, feels like the pulse in your wrist after you’ve let go of someone’s hand. I keep it in a matchbox lined with fallen eyelashes. When you shake it, it sounds like a distant train, or maybe a heartbeat.

I think the language is learning to speak itself back into existence. Last night, I dreamt it whispered my name in the voice of a rusted swing set. Today, I found a new crack in the sky. I’ll fill it with starlight and the leftover glue from childhood.
