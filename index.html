The Bones of My Hands Are Full of Light

*— diary of a potlatch ghost*

Today I give away my shadow. It is not much, but the sun has been hungry. I lay it on the wet stones by the river, where the salmon children sleep. They will need it more than me.

The elders say: *To keep is to starve.* But how do I explain to them that my ribs are already hollow, that my voice is a borrowed cup? Last night, I dreamed of a feast where everyone brought their absence. We ate the empty chairs first. They tasted like salt and unfinished letters.

I have begun to trade my memories. One for a spool of red thread, another for the sound of a crow laughing. The third I gave to a stranger who mistook me for her daughter. Her hands were cold, but her eyes were full of burning houses. I think she was happier with my memory than I was.

The children here collect teeth—not their own, but the ones they find in the moss, the ones the river spits back. They say teeth are the only bones that remember how to sing. I tried to give them my laughter, but it broke in my mouth like a too-thin icicle. They took it anyway.

Sometimes I forget which words are mine. The wind steals them, or maybe I leave them in the pockets of coats I no longer own. Yesterday, I found the word *"belong"* curled under a rock, licking its wounds. I did not take it back.

The potlatch is tomorrow. I will offer my breath. It is a small thing, but the air here is heavy with ungiven gifts. The elders will nod. The river will take what it wants. And I—I will stand at the edge, counting my fingers to make sure I haven’t kept too many.
