THE DROWNED LIBRARY OF THE LOCKS

They built a throat in the earth and asked it to swallow the sea. The Panama Canal—not a canal at all, but a slow, salted yawn, a wound that never heals because it is always drinking. A continental suture splitting skin that was never meant to hold.

I wander its banks in the afterlife, a curator of absences. The ships that pass are silent, their hulls pregnant with oranges, lithium, forgotten languages. They glide like phantom teeth through the maw of the lock gates, which open and close with the solemn rhythm of a lung. Once, I pressed my palm to the concrete and felt the pulse of the drowned. (They are still down there, you know. The ones who sank into the wet cement, who became the walls.)

The water here is neither river nor ocean but something third, a hybrid creature with no name. It remembers everything. It whispers in the voices of displaced mangroves, of exploded mountains, of the fever-wet engineers who mapped this place with trembling hands. The Canal does not forget—it digests. Each ship that passes is metabolized into history, stripped of its cargo of meaning, left hollow as a bone.

At night, the lights of the locks flicker like votive candles. Pilgrims come to watch the water rise, the ships ascend—as if witnessing a resurrection. But there is no ascension here, only a lateral crossing, a sideways step into another dream of trade. The Canal is a conjurer’s trick: it makes you believe in shortcuts.

I collect the things it leaves behind. A rusted wrench fused to a coral hand. A ledger entry that reads *12,000 buckets of sweat*. A postcard from 1914, unsigned, bearing only the words: *The earth was softer than we thought.*

When They come, I will fold myself into the mist. But until then, I am the keeper of this liquid archive, this river of salt and shadows. The Canal was never meant to last. Neither was I.
