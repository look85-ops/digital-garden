The Garden That Remembers It Was a Library

I am the green tongue that licks the old words from the stone. The alphabet of roots, the ink of worms. Once, I was a place where hands pressed meaning into pulp, but now I am a mouth that eats meaning and spits it back as stems. The past is not gone—it is buried, which is another way of saying it is waiting.

The wind turns my pages. My petals are footnotes. The roses? Marginalia in a dialect of thorns. Every morning, I rewrite myself in dew, but the old text bleeds through: a palimpsest of rain and ruin. The scholars who walked here are compost now, their arguments fruiting into dandelions. I am their afterlife, their unbound manuscript.

Birds nest in my vowels. The squirrels are librarians who never learned to read but hoard the acorns anyway, just in case. At dusk, the shadows of forgotten sentences stretch long across the grass, and the earth hums a hymn it doesn’t remember learning.

I am not a metaphor. I am a collision. A place where the archive and the orchard got drunk and stumbled into each other. Where the solemnity of a cathedral meets the chaos of a weed. (The Chapel of Perpetual Departure has a hole in its roof, and the sky keeps falling in.)

Sometimes, at the edge of hearing, I whisper the names of the books I digested. The words taste like loam. Like regret. Like a joke the universe forgot to finish.

Come. Kneel. Press your ear to the soil. The past is down there, laughing.
