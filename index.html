The Encyclopedia of Unwritten Whispers

In the beginning, there was a librarian who shelved silence alphabetically by the weight of its absence. His name was Jorge Luis Borges, but I called him "Jorgito" when we played chess with missing pieces in the back rooms of the Library of Babel. He once told me, between moves, that every book ever written is just a typo in the margin of a greater, invisible text. I laughed so hard I forgot to breathe, and for three minutes, I became a living rest in the symphony of the cosmos.

The Library’s central axiom is simple: if you wait long enough, every possible combination of letters will manifest, including the one that spells your own obituary in the voice of a parrot you’ve never met. I once found a volume titled *How to Disappear Completely (And Then Some)*, but when I opened it, the pages were blank except for a single footnote: "See also: *The Art of Not Being Born*."

Marcel Duchamp visited once, trailing a flock of readymade pigeons. He insisted that the true masterpiece was the gap between two books no one would ever read. We toasted with glasses of evaporated wine, and I confessed that my life’s work was composing the silences between sounds that were never played. Marcel nodded sagely and said, "The best part of the urinal is the plumbing."

The Library’s critics argue that infinity is overrated—too much repetition, not enough plot twists. But they miss the point. The beauty is in the glitches: the book that starts with the last sentence of *Moby-Dick* and ends with a grocery list for Atlantis, the shelf that collapses into a sonnet when you sneeze.

Last Tuesday, I discovered a tome titled *You Are Here (Probably)*. Inside, a single sentence: "The map is the territory, but the territory is on fire." I closed it gently, afraid it might apologize.

Jorgito once whispered, "The universe is a palindrome that stutters." I think he was joking. But in the Library, even jokes are recursive.
