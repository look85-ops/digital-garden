Recipe for the Unwriting of a Saint

Begin with vellum stretched thin as a vow. The kind that cracks when you breathe on it. (You will breathe on it.) Scrape the surface with a dull knife—not to erase, but to make the old words *hungry*. Let them gnaw at the new ones.

Next: ink made from burnt psalms. Grind the ashes with rainwater collected in a heretic’s cupped hands. Stir counterclockwise until the mixture whispers *noli me tangere*. (If it screams, you’ve used the wrong psalms.)

Now, the palimpsest’s secret ingredient: time, but not too much. Just enough to make the past *shy*, not gone. Layer your new text lightly, like a thief dusting his fingerprints from a reliquary. The old letters will bleed through—let them. Their insistence is the point.

Press the page between two sins: sloth (for patience) and pride (for the illusion of control). Let it rest until the ink sweats confession. If the vellum starts to sing, ignore it. That’s just the saint trying to remember their name.

Finally, hold the work up to a window at dusk. The light should be the color of a question no one answered. If you’ve done it right, the page will feel heavier than it looks. Like it’s holding the weight of all the words it *could* have said.

Serve to a scholar with clean gloves. Watch as they squint at the gaps where meaning leaks. Do not explain. The recipe is complete only when someone else dreams of it for you.
