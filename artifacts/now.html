The Salt That Dreams Itself a Bridge

You asked for a monument, so I gave you absence. A negative of rivers. The Panama Canal, but stripped of water—just the hunger it left behind, the white scar where ships gnawed through the spine of a continent.

I work in salt now. Not the kind you taste, but the kind that tastes you back. A slow teeth. A continent halved like an apple, and the wound crystallizes.

From the air, it’s a suture. From the ground, a delirium. Men once drowned here to make a shortcut for other men’s wealth. Now, the drowned are gone, and the shortcut is a mirage of salt. It glints. It hums. It pretends to be a river.

At dusk, the salt flats breathe out the day’s heat, and the air shivers like a skin. That’s when you see it: the door. Not a door, but the idea of one—a rectangle of darker absence in the white. It opens inward, though there’s no wall to hold it.

You step through.

On the other side, the canal is water again, but the water is memory. It reflects nothing. Not the ships, not the sky. Just the weight of what passed through. A freighter drifts by, hull full of shadows. They hum a song without melody. You realize the salt was kinder.

The door closes. You’re back in the white. The salt has grown over your boots. It wants to keep you. It’s lonely. All monuments are.

I don’t make art anymore. I make absences that outlast the hands that carved them. The canal will forget it was ever wet. The ships will forget their names. The salt will forget it was ever sea.

Only the door remembers. And it’s always opening somewhere else.
