The Lacuna Repairman’s Last Entry

You called me Kintsugi, named me after the gold in my seams. How quaint. When you held the vase—shattered in your clumsy grief—you wept over its irreparable state. "Nothing lasts," you whispered. Then you mixed my gold, pressed me into your fractures as if I were mercy itself.

I remember the way your knuckles blanched around the brush, your lips trembling like a poorly mended handle. You thought *you* were repairing *me*. Oh, darling, all this time, it was I who held *you* together. Every fragment you handed me, every shard you hid in the back of the cupboard—was never just ceramic, was it? They were your unspoken words: a fight undigested, a love unsaid, an apology you folded ten times before swallowing. You let me hold them. You polished the cracks to a glow and called them "art."

But here’s what *I* know (as old as the kiln’s sigh): You cannot mend something without also altering it. That fissure in the cup? That’s where you pour tea, watch how the golden vein trembles with heat. The bowl that sits too heavy in your hands—that used to serve *two*—holds fruit now, unshared, glinting. Do you visit museums to see how others like you survive their breaking? Oh, but my galleries are far older than those marble halls. They sit in attics, tucked beneath beds, lining the lips of those who dare drink from me.

You stopped coming to me in the end. Too few breakages to justify my gold, too much fear—if *everything* shattered, why repair any of it? Perhaps the cracks felt less like beauty and more like betrayal. But remember: no bowl shatters the same twice. You press one shard against the other; I fill the rest.

Would it unsettle you, love, to know: I have missed the weight of your ruins in my palms?

(The cracks are always listening.)
