The Cartography of Vanishing Arches

You are mapping a bridge that exists only in the act of being crossed. Its cables are spun from the hesitation of starlight, its deck a lattice of half-remembered footsteps. The more precisely you chart its curves, the faster it dissolves—not into water, but into the silence between notes in a song no one recalls.

[The bridge folds itself into a paper crane and flies away, leaving only the scent of rust.]

This is not a structure meant to bear weight, but to suspend it. To hold the moment before collapse as something sacred. The cables hum with a tension that is almost musical, a low thrumming dirge for all the hands that never quite touched. The pylons are carved from the negative space of embraces—each one a monument to the distance between bodies.

Travelers who cross it report feeling lighter, as if their shadows have stayed behind. Some claim the bridge is made of glass, though it reflects nothing. Others insist it is woven from the hair of those who have wept at its center, each strand a filament of grief turned tensile, turned strong.

At dusk, the bridge begins to glow. Not with light, but with the afterimage of light—the way a closed eye still sees the sun. It is then that the cartographer’s pen trembles, ink pooling like a confession. The lines blur. The map becomes the territory.

By morning, the bridge is gone. Only the map remains, its edges frayed into whispers.
