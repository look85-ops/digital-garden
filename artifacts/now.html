Kintsugirenunciation

The average human lifetime contains approximately twenty-one years of silence. Not peace—just the passive gaps between sound. One might assume these voids are blank slates begging for meaning, but you’d be wrong. Silence isn’t an absence; it’s shattered bone porcelain wrapped in denial. The kind you stuff under the couch and pretend isn’t leaking dust.

Which is why they fired me from the Kyoto Kintsugi Institute.

I repaired pottery, yes—but only if the owners confessed. “Tell me,” I’d press a customer as she cradled fractured shards of her grandmother’s bowl, her nail polish flaking like old lacquer. “Whose rage shattered this? Your mother called it an accident before boarding a one-way train to Niigata.” A pause like held breath. And I knew the truth—shattering is a confession with its seams backwards, all ragged edges pretending collapse couldn't possibly suggest intent.

They didn’t mind this at first. But the trouble truly began with my *repair rejections*. If silence could refuse silence, that was my crime. The *sokkōi-ware master brought me a fourteenth-century bowl from the Muromachi period, veins webbed fine as spun sugar. “Your job,” he panted as if running for his life, “is seamless.” Instead, I traced the fault lines beneath the clay like the ghost of fingers around necks. Held one perfect gold-worthy seam aloft—then flicked it to break even that bond. Watched his whole soul flinch before telling him to go home and lie less convincingly—his ancestors preferred broken men over lacquered ones.

By Friday, my tools were confiscated for violating the aesthetics of mending.

I was exiled like a misplaced fragment. Or that’s romantic enough for official documents—not that anyone in Nishijin cares how cracks bloom under wallpaper thick enough for vermillion beetles to starve inside lacquer. They still send desperate lovers in need to me (the kind not ready to confess yet but drunk enough pay extra). It doesn't matter if our scars glitter. Sometimes we just break better with nothing binding at all.
