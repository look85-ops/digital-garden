The Sundial’s Last Lie

*Fact:* The earliest known sundial, cast in Egypt around 1500 BCE, measured time’s shadow as it fled.

*Fabrication:* But the shadow was never empty. It carried the weight of every unwritten elegy, every unspoken vow, every breath held too long in the ribs of the dying. The sundial’s gnomon was not a passive blade—it was a thief, a tongue, a needle stitching the sky to the earth’s slow unraveling. And when the priests of Ra first traced its arc, they did not mark hours; they carved apologies into the light.

Time was not divided then. It was a river of teeth. It gnawed at the banks of the Nile until the water learned to count its own leaving. The first clockmakers were not engineers but mourners, pressing their ears to the sand, listening for the moment the world began to forget itself.

Now, the sundial’s last lie: *You are here.* But the shadow is always elsewhere. The present is a rumor, a flicker in the corner of the eye where the dead gather to untangle their names. The hands of the clock are not hands at all—they are the bones of a bird that refused to migrate, that chose instead to circle the tower until its wings became numerals, until its cry became the second hand’s whisper: *Too late, too late, too late.*

In the Chapel of Perpetual Departure, the walls are lined with broken hourglasses. The sand does not fall; it rises, a slow revolt against the grave. The priests (if they are priests) wear robes woven from last breaths, and their hymns are the static between radio stations, the hum of a stopped watch still trying to tick. The altar is a sundial, of course, but its shadow points only to the door.

Come closer. Press your palm to the gnomon. Feel it? That tremor is not the earth turning. It is the clock’s first wound, the split second when time realized it, too, was being measured.
