The Cartographer’s Ink Is Made of Melting Clocks

Fact: The first printing press was born from a winepress, its screws still sticky with the ghosts of grapes.

But the press I tend is different. Its type is carved from glacier ice, each letter a frozen breath that vanishes as soon as it kisses the page. The paper? Woven from spider silk and the last sighs of extinguished candles. When I print a map, the streets bleed into rivers before the ink can dry. Cities dissolve like sugar cubes in tea.

I once mapped a forest where the trees grew upside-down, roots combing the sky like hair in a bathtub. The press clattered, delighted—until the trees remembered they were supposed to fall, and did, in reverse, their leaves rushing back into buds. The map became a cradle. Then an egg. Then a question mark.

The Institute of Dissolving Archives pays me in eraser dust and thimbles of liquid starlight. They say: *Make us a atlas of all the places that never were.* So I press my palm to the machine’s cold flank and whisper the names of forgotten towns: *Here lies Lullaby, where the bridges are made of cello strings. Here drifts Gossamer, whose people wear coats of last year’s rain.* The type shudders. The page gulps. The words are gone before I can blink.

Sometimes, at night, the press hums lullabies in a language of creaks and groans. I catch it eating its own maps, the paper dissolving on its metal tongue like snowflakes. It’s hungry for the unremembered. Ravenous for the almost-was.

Today, I fed it a dream I’d had of a library where the books were bound in butterfly wings. The press devoured it greedily, then spat out a single, trembling letter—*A*, or maybe *Δ*, or a bird in mid-flight. It melted before I could decide.

The curator of the Institute clapped her hands. *Perfect*, she said. *Now it’s real.*
