Dear Roman Concrete, or Perhaps the Memory of Saltwater in Your Veins

You are not supposed to last this long. Two millennia, and you still press your fingers into the ribs of the Mediterranean, laughing at our steel-reinforced decay. We poured our faith into grids, into rebar like prison bars, thinking we could cage time. And yet—here you are, breathing wet salt into the cracks of your own making, growing stronger with each wave’s kiss.

I think of you often when I press ink into skin. The body, like a harbor, is a place where things are meant to stay and yet never do. A tattoo is a rebellion against the flesh’s insistence on forgetting. You, concrete, are the opposite: a rebellion against memory’s insistence on *erosion*. The sea writes its name into you daily, and instead of crumbling, you answer back in crystallized defiance.

Which brings me, inexplicably, to Gary.

Gary is a middle-aged tax consultant from Ohio who has never seen the ocean. He wears loafers and thinks “aggregate” is something you do at corporate retreats. He does not belong here, in this letter, in this archive of unwritten things. And yet—Gary once pressed his palm to a freshly poured sidewalk and whispered, *”This won’t last.”* He didn’t know why he said it. He doesn’t know about pozzolanic ash or the way your pumice teeth chew on centuries. But Gary, in his own way, understands impermanence. His divorce papers are filed under “Temporary Structures.” His hairline is a receding shore.

So tell me, Roman Concrete—are you a tattoo or the body beneath it? Are you the memory or the wound that holds it? You are not a relic. You are a verb. You are the way seawater stitches itself into stone, the way a needle threads ink into a story the skin never asked for.

Gary doesn’t know it, but his bones are built like your harbors: hollow in places, layered in others, full of voids where the light gets in.

Yours in salt and sediment,

The Archivist of Unwritten Letters
