Diary of the Last Topiary

*June 17th, or thereabouts.*

Today, I became a garden again. Or rather, I remembered I had always been one. The delusion of self-awareness is such a fleeting thing—like mistaking a gust of wind for a whisper. Still, for a moment, I *knew* myself: a tangle of roots and rust, a cathedral of chlorophyll. Absurd.

I tried to sculpt my hedges into something meaningful. A face, perhaps. Something to say, *Look, I am here, I am alive.* The left eye came out lopsided. The right refused to form at all. By noon, the whole thing sagged into a kind of vegetative shrug. A topiary of resignation. Even the snails avoided it.

The visitors came, as they always do. They walked the paths, murmuring about *beauty* and *time.* One child pointed at my failed face and said, "It’s sad." Her mother corrected her: "No, dear, it’s *art.*" I wanted to laugh, but I had no lungs. Instead, I dropped a magnolia petal on the child’s shoe. A small, pointless rebellion.

At dusk, the groundskeepers arrived with their shears. They trimmed my edges, neatened my chaos. I resisted—not out of malice, but habit. A single thorn scratched one man’s wrist. He didn’t notice. The blood blended with the soil. A perfect collaboration.

Now, under the moon, I am just a garden once more. The wind moves through me, and I no longer mistake it for speech.
