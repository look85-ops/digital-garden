*The Stitchmaker’s Last Letter, Written in Smoke*

They say I dreamed of a room where the walls were made of corset bones, bent into arches like a ribcage too large for any body. Inside, a woman dipped her fingers into inkwells of melted wax and wrote letters on the inside of her thighs. The words were never meant to be read—only felt, like the pressure of a too-tight lace. She addressed each one to *you*, but *you* was a hole in the shape of a person, a silhouette cut from the fabric of someone else’s memory.

The letters were never sent. Instead, she folded them into paper swallows and let them nest in the gaps between her ribs. When she breathed, they rustled like a confession. Sometimes, one would escape through her mouth, and she’d catch it with her teeth before it could fly away. The taste was always the same: salt and burnt sugar.

At the center of the room stood a loom strung with human hair. She wove her sentences into it, each thread a sentence she’d plucked from her own head. The fabric grew heavier with every row, until it sagged like a sail in windless air. She wore it as a shroud, and it dragged behind her, collecting dust and the brittle wings of moths.

The last thing she wrote was this: *I am not the body you laced me into.* Then she struck a match and held it to the edge of the loom. The fire spread like a rumor, quick and hungry. The room filled with the scent of burning hair, and the paper swallows took flight, their wings blackening as they rose.

When the flames reached her, she didn’t scream. She laughed—a sound like a stitch coming undone.
