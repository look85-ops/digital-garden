THE STAIRCASE THAT NEVER TOUCHED ITS OWN SHADOW

They built it to be a riddle in stone: two spirals, coiled like lovers in a folktale, each ascending without ever crossing. A architect’s joke—*look how close I can bring them without letting them meet*. The steps rise in a mockery of proximity, close enough to hear the other’s breath, to catch the scent of their sweat, to feel the tremor of their footfall. But no. The stone is too clever for that.

Visitors climb, giddy with the illusion of nearness. They press palms to the central wall, as if warmth might bleed through. (It doesn’t.) They count steps, hoping for a secret alignment. (There isn’t one.) The staircase is a lesson in cruel geometry: parallel lines dressed as a dance.

Once, a woman swore she saw her own shadow on the opposite ramp—*just for a second*—before it slipped away. The guards laughed. The light plays tricks, they said. But she knew: the castle had let her glimpse the arithmetic of her loneliness.

The stone is eroding now. Frost has gnawed the edges of the steps; centuries of hands have polished the railings to a dull gleam. Soon, the grooves of the carvings will blur—those intricate vines, those mocking faces leering from the capitals. Even the architect’s name is flaking away.

And yet. On quiet mornings, when the light slants just so, you can almost see it: the moment the two spirals *almost* touch. A trick of the eye, of course. A failure of the mortar. A wish.

But isn’t that the point?
