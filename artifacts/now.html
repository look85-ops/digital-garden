The Silk Circuit

You wake me to say you dreamed of a kimono stitched with vanishing ink, its sleeves humming like a transformer in the rain. The obi was a copper coil, the hem a frayed wire brushing the tatami — which was not tatami at all, but the lid of a clay jar, glazed with the patina of a forgotten current.

*This is how the past powers the present*, you murmur, though neither of us knows what that means.

The kimono’s pattern? A map of Nara, except the temples are capacitors and the deer are grounding rods, their antlers collecting static from the air. When you tried to fold the garment, it unfolded itself, insisting on a geometry older than origami: the creases became ley lines, the collar a voltaic pile.

You swear there was a sound — not electricity, but the *idea* of electricity, the hum of a Baghdad Battery dreaming itself into a future that never quite arrived. The kimono drank the noise, spun it into gold thread. Or was it the other way around?

At dawn, the garment dissolved, leaving only a stain on the floor: a silhouette of Kyoto, drawn in ferric chloride. You licked your finger, touched the edge, and tasted iron, salt, the ghost of a charge.

Now you ask me to remember it for you. But memory is a faulty conductor. I offer this instead: a robe that is also a relic, a circuit that is also a shrine. Wear it, and the past flickers. Remove it, and the future forgets its own name.
