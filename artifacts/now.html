The Ceremony of the Unspent Coin

Imagine a city where every transaction is an apology. Citizens shuffle through fog-lit markets, their pockets heavy with coins they refuse to spend. Instead, they press them into each other’s palms like contraband love letters, whispering, *"This is not for you, but because of you."* The economy runs on guilt, a currency so volatile it inflates when ignored.

At the center of the city stands the *Monument to the First Refusal*, a towering helix of melted-down pennies, each one etched with the face of someone who once said, *"No, keep it."* Historians debate whether it’s a celebration or a warning. The plaque reads: *"Here lies the weight of generosity unreciprocated—may it never settle."* (1)

Gifts here are never given directly. A loaf of bread appears on your windowsill, still warm, wrapped in a map of places you’ll never visit. By dawn, it’s gone, replaced by a single glove. You wear it for a week before realizing it’s yours—left on a bench years ago. The glove, now slightly mildewed, is a palimpsest of your own neglect.

The mayor delivers speeches in reverse, unraveling promises before they’re made. *"I will not build you a bridge,"* she declares, and the crowd cheers as the blueprint dissolves in the river. Infrastructure is always speculative: a staircase that ends mid-air, a library where the books are all dedications (*"For A., who hates surprises"*).

Children are taught the *Liturgy of the Unseen Hand*, a dance where they mimic the act of dropping something precious, then pretending not to notice. The highest compliment is to be called *"a well-placed shadow."*

Critics argue the system is unsustainable. *"You can’t fuel a society on passive-aggressive altruism,"* wrote one dissident, shortly before being buried in anonymous thank-you notes.

At dusk, the streets hum with the sound of coins being polished—not for spending, but for the moment they’ll be mistaken for moonlight and slipped into a stranger’s coat pocket.

(1) *From "The Atlas of Unfinished Transactions," Vol. XII: "Debt as a Form of Weather"*
