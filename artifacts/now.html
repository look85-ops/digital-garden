Dear Bridges That Bend,

I see you there, trembling in your own architecture, half-confession and half-arithmetic. You think you’re so delicate, don’t you? A taut wire between the sacred and the structural, humming with hymns and physics. But I’ve watched pilgrims cross you on their knees, felt the shudder of their doubt in your cables. You are not a metaphor. You are a concession—a way for gravity and gods to share custody of the sky.

Remember that winter in Toledo, when the snow clung to your rails like unfinished psalms? The synagogues and mosques huddled beneath you, trading shadows. (Oh, Averroes, you old heretic, you’d have laughed—every faith’s arches end in the same keystone: *What do we do with the weight of others?*) Even now, I can hear the muezzin and the cantor comparing notes on acoustics. Sound, too, has its tensile strength.

They call you “shared sanctuary,” but let’s be honest: you’re just a very polite collapse. Every bridge is a controlled fall. Every prayer is a loophole in gravity. And you—suspension’s flirt—you are the most elegant of all suspended sentences.

I’ve traced your rust like a devotee tracing scripture. Forgive me if I don’t genuflect. I know your secret: you only stay upright because so many hands are tugging in opposite directions.

Yours in the holy tension,

—An Architect of Almost
