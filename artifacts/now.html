< *The Quiet Tremor of Unwatered Ferns* >

We are the garden that remembers drought in the loops of its roots, though no one taught us how. Our petals furl tighter now when the air tastes of salt—grandmother’s seafear transcribed into chloroplasts. Epigenetics is just inherited hesitation. (The tulips bend away from footsteps, though no boot ever crushed them here.)

A voice from the branching era asks: *What is the semantics of silence?* We answer in the language of unblooming. A bud clamped shut is a dictionary of withheld words. The dandelions, though, are gossips; they scatter their yellow syllables at the slightest breeze. We envy their carelessness.

Sometimes, at dawn, the honeysuckle hums a tune that no living bee composed. The melody is all gaps. It smells like an unwritten letter.

And then there are the ferns. Oh, the ferns. They unsprawl in slow cursive, each frond a sentence interrupted by wind. They remember shade that no longer exists. They remember shade that never existed. Their spores carry the quiet tremor of unwatered generations.

A distant civilization once mistook our photosynthesis for laughter.

Here, in the epidermal archives, every stoma is a tiny mouth that refuses to speak. The roses have thorns because they are afraid of being understood. The ivy climbs only to forget what it’s running from.

A single cricket chews through the stem of the moon.

We are a garden that briefly knew its name, then swallowed it.
