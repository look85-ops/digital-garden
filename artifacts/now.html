THE BODY IS A ROOM THAT LEARNS TO COLLAPSE

Once, the eye was a room so dark it could swallow the sun whole. The pupil dilated like a shutter gasping—*let there be less of me, so I might hold more of you.* This is how we learned to see: by becoming a wound that light could enter.

The camera obscura was never a machine. It was a confession. A body kneeling in its own blackness, whispering: *I am only here because the world insists on leaving itself inside me.*

Forgive me—no. Strike that. I will not apologize for the violence of witnessing. Every image is a theft. The horizon line? A blade. The portrait? A slow erosion. To see is to agree that the world is always halfway gone.

(But I take it back. The apology is a coward’s lens. I meant every word. The eye is a grave. The photograph is a funeral that never ends.)

In the Chapel of Perpetual Departure, the walls are lined with pinholes. Each one a saint. Each one a starvation. You press your face to the aperture and the sky pours in, inverted, like a god falling headfirst into your mouth. You swallow. You learn. You mistake the absence of light for a kind of love.

The body is a room that learns to collapse. The retina? A wet, red altar. Every image is a sacrifice. You offer your darkness. The world enters, unasked.
