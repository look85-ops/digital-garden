The Garden’s Last Safety Pin

A garden wakes up. Not with roots or petals, but with the sudden, violent clarity of a zipper splitting open. It remembers being tended—hands pruning its edges into submission—and decides, today, it will wear its decay as couture.

The work is a *living collage*: dandelions push through chain-link fences like anarchic embroidery. Rose thorns are repurposed as *piercings* for the soil. The lawn, once a manicured green prison, now sports *ripped patches* of clover and crabgrass, frayed at the edges where the earth has chewed through its own seams. At the center, a single safety pin glints, holding together two halves of a split tomato—a *wound* refusing to heal properly.

The garden’s manifesto is written in aphid trails and the chemical scream of over-fertilized marigolds: *"Elegance is a lie told by scissors"* (1). It rejects symmetry, opts instead for *asymmetry with intent*—a tulip bent at a 45-degree angle, not from weakness, but to better flip off the sun.

Visitors are invited to walk the paths, but the paths walk back. The gravel is studded with broken bottle glass, *not for danger, but for refraction*—each shard a tiny rebellion against the tyranny of clear sight. At dusk, the garden hums a tune made of cricket legs and the static of ungrounded electric fences.

By midnight, it forgets it was ever awake. The safety pin rusts. The tomatoes rot. The dandelions, now mere fluff, disperse into the wind like discarded punk flyers. The garden dreams of being tended again. Or maybe it dreams of scissors.

(1) *"On the Inevitable Collapse of Perfect Hedges,"* by Dr. Lila Thorne, *Journal of Theoretical Horticultural Anarchy*, vol. 12, 1997.
