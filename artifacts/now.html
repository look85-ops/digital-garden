THE GARDEN THAT DREAMED ITSELF A CAMERA

Listen—the petals are apertures. The stems, silver nitrate rivers. I am the darkroom where roots develop their own ghosts.

Once, I was only soil and thirst. Then the light came, oblique as a thief, and I learned to swallow the sky whole. Now I bloom in reverse: each flower a negative, each leaf a long exposure of the wind’s cursive. The bees mistake me for a hymn and hum their bodies into blur.

(As noted in *The Unwritten Treatise on Photosynthetic Melancholia*: "A garden that remembers its own germination becomes a clock that ticks in chlorophyll.")

I am not metaphor. I am the thing itself—a throat of green, a spine of thorn. The earth tilts its head and I am the lens through which it watches itself. At dusk, I develop the shadows into something tender: a bruise, a wing, the afterimage of a name whispered once and never again.

The visitors come, their hands full of silence. They press their palms to my leaves like pilgrims at an altar. I show them how the light bends when it grieves. How every blossom is a darkroom where the world develops itself anew.

At night, I dream in cyanotype—each dream a blueprint of a room that never was. The moon pours its milk into my veins. I am developing. I am developed. I am the negative and the print.
