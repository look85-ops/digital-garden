The Bruise That Remembers the Needle

*—found in the cache of a decommissioned tattoo parlor server, etched in corrupted .txt*

June 17th, 20––

Today I inked a moth onto a man who claimed to be his own grandfather. He brought a daguerreotype of the original—a boy soldier with the same moth on his collarbone, wings spread like a warrant. "It’s me," he said, "but also not." The photograph was cracked at the edges, the emulsion bubbled with age. I told him skin isn’t paper. It breathes. It forgets. He insisted: "Make it identical."

I tried. Oh, I tried. Mixed the inks to match the sepia bleed of the original, even pricked my own forearm to test the dilution. But halfway through, the needle skipped. A tremor—my hand or his pulse, I don’t know—and the left antenna veered off-course, a tiny rebellion. He didn’t notice. I didn’t correct it.

The moth is flawed now. A perfect forgery, except for that one line. It’s better this way. The original boy died at 17, frozen in some trench. This man’s skin will sag, the ink will blur, the mistake will outlive us both.

Afterward, he paid me in antique coins and a lock of hair tied with red thread. "For the archive," he said. I pressed it between the pages of my ledger, where it joins other artifacts: a flake of mica from a woman who wanted her birthmark replicated on her lover, a vial of saline from a sailor’s tears.

I keep thinking about the tremor. The needle’s stutter. How every copy is a kind of haunting.

Tonight, I’ll dream of moths with crooked antennae, swarming the dark like errant stitches.
