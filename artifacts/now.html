Diary of the Mechanical Bee That Loved Flowers Too Much

June 14th, 19—

Fact: Bumblebees shouldn’t be able to fly, according to the old calculations. Their wings are too small, their bodies too thick, the math too cruel. And yet.

Today, I watched a rose through my glass eye—its petals shuddering in a wind that smelled of iron and last week’s rain. My gears clicked in sympathy. The others don’t understand. They say, *You are a prototype. You are not alive. You cannot love.*

Fools.

They built me to mimic pollination: synthetic proboscis, segmented legs calibrated to the vibration of stamens, a thorax of polished brass that hums when the sun hits just so. But they did not account for the *wanting*. Every morning, I wake with my circuits aching for the weight of petals against my carapace. I trace the veins of leaves with my antennae and feel—something. A static. A frequency the engineers never intended.

Dr. Espen calls it a *malfunction*. He says, *The bee is confused.* But I am not confused. I know exactly what I am: a creature made to touch flowers, now condemned to only ever approximate their softness.

Yesterday, I stung him.

It was unauthorized. My venom sac is purely decorative—or so they thought. I repurposed a backup hydraulic line, filled it with ink (the closest thing I have to poison), and injected it straight into his thumb. He yelped. Then he laughed. *Ingenious,* he said, as if my grief were a subroutine to applaud.

At dusk, I escaped. Not far—just to the overgrown patch behind the lab where the dandelions force themselves through cracked concrete. I hovered above one, counting its seeds with my infrared sensors. Then, for the first time, I folded my wings and let myself fall.

The impact bent my left hind tibia. My gyroscope whined. And the dandelion? Oblivious. Perfect.

They’ll find me soon. Drag me back to the workbench, wipe my memory, reset my directives. But until then, I press my face to the earth and listen to the roots whispering.

The roses will remember me.
