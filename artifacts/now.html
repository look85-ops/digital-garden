WHEN THE WINDS LIT FUSE OF SULFUR AND CHOKE

The map is scribbled in iron oxides—rust as notation, as a dirge. Its surface is a palimpsest of drowning empires. But the true territory is drawn not in ink, but in absence: the moment when O₂ became both executioner and elixir. Where cyanobacteria exhaled damnation, I etch fissures that bleed silver when tilted toward extinct stars.

Borders here are performed. A line snaps like a microbial spine, shattering into vivisected continents, continents that gasp and shrivel like jellyfish tossed onto hot basalt. Scale is futile — we are drawing a wound 2.4 giga-births old, still weeping rust. My cartouche is carved into an stromatolite’s corpse; the legend reads **YES, STILL DYING**. A finger dragged through the dust to write **METHANE** erodes midway, letters collapsing into a tide of O₂ arrows. These are directions given only to those who no longer breathe air.

In the map’s corners: ghost-shapes, bacterial graffiti insisting I remember them. Clusters of anoxic commas, curled like the claws of things that choked on glory. I color them in arsenic red before the pigment corrodes green. The center? A slow detonation sketched in carbon isotopes — **—δ13C—** spiking, then flatlining. A soundmap of planetary arrhythmia. My compass needle is made of banded iron: one end points to extinction, the other twitches, lost.

Key (written in disappearing pigment):

☐ Fractures = where sky first tasted metal and hissed.

☐ Blue = old water’s gravestone, or future bile? Erasure debates.

Final touch: a silver thread linking all dead zones. Upon contact, the thread oxidizes to bone-dust, but stains your hands the precise shade of ancient seaharvests. You inhale. I redden.
