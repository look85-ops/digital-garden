The Museum of Unswallowed Pills

*(The title appears, then vanishes, like a sugar cube dissolving on the tongue of a skeptical child.)*

We once exhibited a glass vial labeled *"Dust from the Wings of Conviction."* Visitors reported a 37% increase in shoulder pain relief after viewing it—until someone pointed out the label was a typo. It should have read *"Dust from the Wings of Conviction* **Moths**.*"* The effect collapsed. This is the curse of the placebo: it thrives only in the unlit corners of the mind, where doubt cannot follow.

A famous study gave patients sham knee surgery. They sliced the skin, then sewed it shut—no intervention, only theater. The patients walked better. The body is a credulous audience, eager to applaud even when the stage is empty. But try explaining that to the knees. They’ll scoff. *"We felt the scalpel,"* they’ll insist. *"We heard the sutures sing."*

In our archives, we keep a vial of *"Tears of a Statue."* The label claims they were harvested from a weeping Virgin Mary in Portugal, 1932. The liquid is saline. The cork is glued shut. The real miracle is how many visitors leave clutching their ribs, whispering *"I tasted copper."*

Here’s the secret: belief is a kind of vandalism. It scrapes away the original paint and replaces it with something brighter, something that *should* have been there all along. The body is merely the canvas.

Our final exhibit is a single white pill on a pedestal. The placard reads: *"This cures nothing. You will take it anyway."* Visitors lean in, squinting. Some laugh. Some tremble. One woman fainted. The pill is aspirin. Or maybe chalk. Or maybe it’s the idea of aspirin, wrapped in the skin of chalk—a matryoshka doll of expectation.

We close early on Tuesdays. The artifacts need time to doubt themselves.
