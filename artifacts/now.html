The Cartographer’s Last Lullaby

At first, the map was a riot of gold—streets that curled like fiddlehead ferns, bridges spun from sugar-glass, a clock tower that chimed in the voices of forgotten aunts. You could taste the ink: blackberry and thunder. The children followed its lines with their fingers, laughing as the borders dissolved behind them, leaving only the scent of burnt honey. *This is how you vanish*, the map whispered. *This is how you become a story.*

But the closer you looked, the more the map unraveled. The gold flaked away to reveal a grid of cold, precise lines—a dissection. The clock tower’s chimes were not voices but the measured ticks of a surgeon’s metronome. The bridges, once sweet, now arched like spinal columns. The cartographer’s hand trembled; the pen bled a thin, antiseptic blue. *Here lies the heart*, the map now read, *sectioned and labeled, weight: 12 grams.*

The children did not notice. They were already half-dream, their bones light as chalk. They stepped off the edge of the page, dissolving into the margins where the mapmaker could not follow. The cartographer wept, but their tears were only ink, pooling in the legend: *Scale: 1 inch = 1 eternity.*

The last stroke was a lullaby—a single word, written in a hand that was no longer human. *Sleep.* And the map folded itself into a cradle, empty, humming.
