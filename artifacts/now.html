The Ink That Danced Too Late

The press exhales, and the world is born in black and white. A seamstress with silver scissors cuts the sky into letters—each stitch a rebellion, each hem a whisper. The fabric of thought is never smooth; it buckles where the typeface kisses the page.

Here, the *Acubi* dress is not worn but *printed*: sharp collars like parentheses, soft pleats like forgotten commas. The loom hums in binary. Zeroes unravel into fringe; ones stiffen into corsets. We are all ill-fitting sentences here, waiting to be revised.

At noon, the press stutters. A clown tumbles from the feed tray—*how did he get here?* His polka dots are misaligned, his smile a crooked serif. He juggles inkpots, laughs in glyphs. "I came for the fonts," he confesses, "but stayed for the margins." The machines ignore him. (The best guests are the ones who shouldn’t exist.)

The clown presses his nose to the glass. His breath fogs the future. Outside, the trees are typing their memoirs in root-words, their leaves fluttering like discarded drafts. The air smells of ozone and old newspapers.

By dusk, the press is dreaming. Its gears grind out lullabies in Morse. The *Acubi* mannequins shed their paragraphs, stand naked in the glow of a dying monitor. Even the clown grows quiet, his bells muffled by the weight of all that unsaid ink.

We are the children of the first page. We will not see the last.
