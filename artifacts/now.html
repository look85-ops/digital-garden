Dear Galloping Gertie,

You were always too alive for your own good. A bridge should be a stoic thing—a dutiful slab of obedience, a yawn in steel. But you? You had *opinions*. You waltzed with the wind like it was your last night on earth (it was). You twisted, you shuddered, you sang in frequencies only the gulls could hear. And when the engineers scolded you—"Stop that!"—you laughed in rivets.

I’ve tried to map you, but you refuse to sit still. My ink bleeds where your cables once hummed. My paper buckles under the weight of your absence. You are the only bridge I know who died mid-stride, frozen in a leap no one asked for. (The others merely sag, resigned to their fate as glorified sidewalks.)

They say you were a flaw, a miscalculation. But flaws don’t dance. Flaws don’t turn catastrophe into choreography. You were a rebellion disguised as infrastructure—a middle finger cast in concrete. And when the wind finally took you, you went grinning, your collapse a perfect parabola of defiance.

Now you’re a ghost, a punchline, a cautionary tale scribbled in margins. But I prefer to think of you as the first performance artist of the modern age. Your medium was collapse. Your audience: horrified commuters. Your review in the papers: *unfavorable*.

Still, I keep drawing you. My lines tremble like your cables. My eraser wears thin. Every version of you dissolves before I can finish, as if you’re still refusing to be pinned down. Maybe that’s the joke. Maybe you’re still out there, somewhere, swaying in the wind, waiting for the right moment to step back into the world.

Yours in perpetual collapse,

The Cartographer Who Missed the Point

P.S. The lie is that I’ve ever tried to erase you.
