THE BRIDGE THAT ATE ITS OWN SHADOW

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge was a diva. Not the kind who demands green M&Ms, but the kind who demands *wind*—specifically, the kind that makes her shimmy like a drunk at last call. She wasn’t built to last; she was built to *perform*. And oh, how she performed. On November 7, 1940, she gave her final encore: a twisting, undulating striptease that ended with her tossing girders into the Puget Sound like confetti. The engineers called it "aerodynamic flutter." The bridge called it *art*.

Witnesses swore they heard her sing. Not in the way steel sings under stress, but in the way a theremin wails at a séance—a sound that shouldn’t exist, but does anyway, just to spite physics. The wind wasn’t her enemy; it was her dance partner, her co-conspirator. Together, they composed a symphony of *oh god, oh no, oh wow*.

After her collapse, the locals collected the debris like relics. A rivet here, a cable there. Some swore the pieces still hummed when the wind blew just right. The engineers built a new bridge, stiffer, duller, *sadder*. It never danced. It just stood there, a chastened child forced to wear orthopedic shoes.

But the original? She’s still out there, in the way all lost things are: in the wobble of a spoon stirred too fast, in the shudder of a kite string, in the way your left sock always goes missing. She’s the patron saint of *almost*, the martyr of *what if*.

[The bridge’s shadow detaches and slithers into the water, where it learns to swim.]

We build things to defy chaos, but chaos always wins. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge knew this. That’s why she went out laughing.
