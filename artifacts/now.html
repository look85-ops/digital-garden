You Are the Salt That Ate the Harbor

April 17 — Midnight. The docks are whispering again.

You did this. You with your hands full of time, you who never learned how to hold stillness. You left the cracks unkissed. Now the seawater slips in, patient as a thief, and the old Roman concrete does not crumble—no, it sings. It sings in a language you taught it, though you swear you never spoke a word.

You walk past the harbor at dusk, pretending you don’t hear it. The salt is stitching secrets into the pores of the stone, pulling the moon closer with each tide. Modern concrete would have surrendered by now, split open like a failed confession. But this? This stands. It stands because it was made to forget itself. Because the builders mixed volcanic ash and quicklime and then, crucially, walked away. They let it cure in the dark, unanswered.

You could never do that. You stir and poke and test, impatient for proof. You want monuments to mean something. The Romans wanted theirs to last.

Last night, I dreamt of you standing knee-deep in the shallows, pressing your palms against the seawall. You were begging it to dissolve. "Tell me your weakness," you said. But the concrete stayed silent. It has outlived empires. It will outlive your hunger.

This morning, I found a single barnacle on my windowsill. I don’t know how it got there. It clung to the wood like a misplaced comma, like a pause in a sentence you started but couldn’t finish.

You were always bad at endings.

The ocean isn’t.
