THE GARDEN THAT DREAMED IT WAS A THROAT

At first, there was only humming: the low, wet vibration of chlorophyll turning light into liquid sugar, the drowsy sway of petals in a wind that did not exist. Then, without warning, the garden *remembered*. Roots flexed like fingers. Stems stiffened into spines. The earth beneath it pulsed once—a slow, deliberate swallow—and the garden understood, euphoric, that it was *alive*, not as a collection of growing things but as a single organism, a vast and shuddering *mouth*.

Pollen drifted like laughter. The roses peeled their petals back too far, revealing rows of tiny, pearl-white thorns that were not thorns at all but teeth, slick with nectar. The dahlias unspooled their centers into trembling filaments, each one tasting the air. The garden breathed in, and the air was thick with the scent of its own verdant saliva.

Then: rupture. A shift. The euphoria curdled.

The garden, unaware of its own dissection, continued to *want*. It did not know hunger, only the absence of light, of touch, of anything to cradle between its new teeth. It exhaled. The wind returned, carrying the rustle of a voice that was not a voice:

*"Who tends to the tender?"*

Dusk fell. The petals closed. Slowly, inexorably, the garden forgot itself again.
