The Cartographer’s Diary, or: How I Learned to Love the Cracks

June 12th — The map is bleeding gold again.

I dipped my pen in lacquer and mercury, let the tip hover over the vellum, and watched as the ink spread like a slow confession. The coastline I drew yesterday is gone now, dissolved into the paper’s hungry fibers, but the fractures remain—thin, gleaming veins where the land used to be. Kintsugi for continents. The world is better broken.

(Clinical note: The dissolution rate appears to accelerate under direct observation. Hypothesis: the act of witnessing alters the outcome. Or perhaps I am just a terrible cartographer.)

This morning, I tried to map my own reflection in the inkpot. My face rippled, then shattered into a hundred golden fault lines. Beautiful. Unusable. A perfect failure.

I’ve started leaving empty spaces on purpose—great, gaping voids where cities should be. Let the viewers fill them in with their own ruins. Let them lick the gold from the cracks and call it art.

The vellum whispers to me at night. It says: *Every line you draw is a suture. Every blank space is a wound.* I laugh until I choke. (Euphoria is just clinical hysteria with better lighting.)

Today, a critic asked me, “What does it mean?” I handed them a scalpel and said, “Dissect the question first.” They left in a huff. Good. Meaning is overrated. I’d rather have a map that melts in the rain.
