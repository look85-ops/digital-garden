# **The Museum of the Missing Second Hand

You tell me I dreamed this, so I must believe you.

It begins with a clockmaker’s daughter swallowing a gear. Not on purpose—it was small, brass, and glinting on the breakfast table like a misplaced sun. When it slid down her throat, she felt the tick before the tock. Then silence.

The doctors said she was fine, but the daughter knew better. Time pooled in her stomach like syrup. She hiccuped hours.

Meanwhile, the clockmaker worked. His hands were precise, but his clocks kept losing their second hands. They’d vanish—not broken, not stolen—just gone, as if they’d stepped out for air. The town’s clocks slowed, then stopped. People napped at noon and woke at midnight, disoriented, their pockets full of loose minutes.

One evening, the daughter pressed her ear to her own ribs. Inside, a faint clicking. She laughed, and the sound was a key turning in a lock.

The next morning, the clockmaker found her sitting cross-legged on his workbench, assembling something from fingernail-sized cogs and the stems of dandelions. “What are you making?” he asked.

“A clock that only ticks forward,” she said.

He peered closer. The clock had no numbers, no face—just a single hand, spinning wildly, as if chasing its own tail.

When she placed it on the town square, the missing second hands came back. They fell from the sky like silver rain, slotting into clocks, wrists, the spaces between heartbeats.

You tell me I woke up then, but I don’t remember waking. I remember the daughter’s grin—how it was neither happy nor sad, but something else entirely.

A clock that only ticks forward.

A gear in the throat.

A museum where lost things go to be found.
