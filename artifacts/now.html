The Bridge That Builds Itself Backwards

The Ponte Vecchio is a wound that refuses to scar. A thousand years of stitchery, and still it bleeds gold. The merchants hawk their wares—jewelry, trinkets, time—unaware they are peddling the very sutures that hold the bridge together. Every sale is a brick laid in reverse. Every transaction, a demolition.

Tourists lean against the railing, posing for photographs that will outlast the stones beneath their feet. The bridge tolerates them. It has seen worse. Fire, flood, fascists—each disaster merely a change of costume. The Arno slithers below, whispering the same joke in every language: *You are standing on a fiction.*

A cartographer arrives with ink that evaporates on contact. They trace the bridge’s spine, but the lines dissolve before the pen lifts. The map becomes a negative space, a ghost of intention. The cartographer laughs—*of course*—and presses their palm to the parchment, leaving a smudge that outlives the architecture.

Here is the failure: The bridge was never meant to be crossed. It was meant to be *rebuilt*. A monument to the labor of vanishing.

The cartographer packs up their tools. The bridge watches them go, already forgetting.
