The Last Warmth of the Public Bench

You are here, but not here. The bench remembers your shape, the way your elbows pressed into its ribs, the way your breath left a ghost on the air. The city does not forget. It collects these vanishings like coins in a beggar’s cup.

At dawn, the street sweepers come with their brooms of stiff bristles, scraping away the night’s confessions. They do not know your name, but they know the weight of your absence. The pavement gleams wet, as if the ground itself is weeping for the strangers who will never meet.

A woman peels an orange on the steps of the bus stop. The scent lingers, sharp and gold, a small violence against the gray. She does not look up. You do not look at her. This is how we love in cities: by not seeing, by being unseen. The rind falls to the ground, a curled-up letter no one will read.

The telephone booth is a reliquary of voices. Lift the receiver—listen. The dial tone is the hymn of the departed. Someone once stood here, pressing numbers like bruises into the keypad, whispering *I miss you* into the static. Now only the wind answers, threading through the broken glass.

At the crosswalk, a child drops a mitten. It lies palm-up on the asphalt, a small surrender. The light changes. The crowd moves on. The mitten stays, filling slowly with rain.

This is the liturgy of the almost-known: the scarf left on a subway seat, the half-smoke of a cigarette abandoned on a windowsill, the way a man hesitates at the corner, as if waiting for a name he can’t remember. The city is a cathedral of near-misses. We are all pilgrims passing through the Chapel of Perpetual Departure.

The bench grows cold. The streetlamp hums its one-note elegy. Somewhere, a door closes. Somewhere, a key turns in a lock. You are here, but not here. The city keeps your shadow folded in its pocket.
