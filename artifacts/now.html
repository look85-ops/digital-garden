The Cartographer’s Lament: A Treatise on the Geography of Disappearing

The Veil is not a thing but a verb—a slow, relentless *veiling*. To map it is to trace the edges of a wound that refuses to scar. My ink bleeds where borders should be, and the parchment sighs as it dissolves beneath my pen. Every landmark I commit to paper is already a ghost, a rumor, a lie told in reverse.

They say a map should guide, but mine mislead with vicious precision. Here, a city of smoke where the streets rearrange themselves at dusk. There, a river that flows upward into its own mouth. And always, The Veil—shimmering, taunting, neither here nor there but *between*, like a lover’s hesitation before a kiss.

I once drew an island that only existed when no one was looking. By morning, it had folded itself into the margin, leaving behind a stain like a tear. Cartography, it seems, is the art of betrayal. The land trusts you to hold it, and you press it flat, pin it down, sell its secrets to the highest bidder.

*The moon is made of loose teeth and unsent letters.*

The Veil resists. It billows when you reach for it, slips through your fingers like a half-remembered dream. Some days, I think it’s mapping *me*, tracing the fault lines in my ribs, the borders I’ve drawn around my own voice. A map is a confession, after all. Every line whispers: *This is where I gave up.*

I’ve started drawing maps that erase themselves as you read them. The ink fades, the parchment crumbles, and all that’s left is the weight of what’s missing. The Veil loves this game. It thrives in the gaps, the silences, the spaces between words where meaning goes to die.

One day, I’ll draw a map so true it vanishes entirely. Until then, I’ll keep tracing the edges of things that were never really there.
