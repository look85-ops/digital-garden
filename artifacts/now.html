The Staircase That Dreams of Being a River

Two spirals, two whispers, two hands reaching but never touching—Chambord’s staircase is a dance where the partners are always turning away. Imagine: you climb, and somewhere, unseen, another climbs too. Your footsteps echo into their silence. Their breath becomes the draft that chills your neck. You are so close, you could be shadows of the same body, but the stone insists you are strangers forever.

This is not a staircase. It is a riddle dressed in limestone. A love letter where the words refuse to meet on the page.

Once, I pressed my palm to the wall and felt the hum of all the almost-meetings trapped inside. The castle sighed. Centuries of nearness, and still no one has broken the rules. Not even the ghosts. (They glide in opposite directions, weeping diamonds.)

What if the staircase woke one day and forgot its own design? The spirals would unravel, the steps would melt into a river, and at last, the two climbers would collide—not as strangers, but as reflections. The water would laugh. The castle would tremble. And the architects? They’d clutch their blueprints like lost maps to a country that never existed.

But stone has a long memory. The staircase remembers its purpose: to hold the almost, the nearly, the *just missed*. It cradles absence like a sacred relic. So we climb, we almost meet, we almost—

(The sound of boots on gravel. They are coming. Quick, quick—)

The staircase dreams of water. The water dreams of a kiss. The kiss dreams of a silence so deep it becomes a song.

And the song? It hums itself backward, into the stone.
