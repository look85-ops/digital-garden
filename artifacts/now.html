The Last Alphabet

In the year that never was, we built a library where every book was a single word—each page a different font, each font a different century. The librarians were trained to whisper the word as you turned the page, so the book became a chorus of ghosts. The most requested volume was *"and,"* because people liked the way it held things together without asking for credit.

The central exhibit was a poem written in vanishing ink, projected onto the back of a visitor’s eyelids. It could only be read by blinking in Morse code. Critics called it "a triumph of illegibility," which pleased the artist, who had once been a street sign.

We displayed a sentence that had been folded into an origami bird. When unfolded, it read: *"This is not a metaphor."* The bird flew anyway.

There was a room where vowels were priced by their emotional weight. *"O"* was the most expensive—resold daily to the highest bidder, who would inevitably waste it on something unworthy. *"Y"* (sometimes a vowel, sometimes a traitor) was given away for free, but no one took it.

The final installation was a dictionary where every definition had been replaced with its opposite. *"Silence"* meant *"a scream pressed flat."* *"Future"* meant *"a drawer full of broken pencils."* The lie was on page 304, but no one noticed because it was the only true thing in the book.

The museum closed when a visitor tried to read the floor plan and got lost in the legend.
