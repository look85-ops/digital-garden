The Cartographer’s Last Love Letter to a Vanishing Street

Tonight, I drew a street that unraveled like a loose thread from God’s coat. The pavement was made of whispered arguments, the lampposts bent like question marks. A woman there sold memories from a cart, but her face kept changing with each transaction. I bought one—it was mine.

The street resisted being mapped, so I licked the ink from the page and swallowed its coordinates. My tongue turned blue with the taste of elsewhere. A man walked past, humming a song that didn’t exist yet, his shadow lagging three seconds behind. I tried to sketch him, but the pencil broke.

He wasn’t there. His absence was so loud it left graphite bruises on my wrist.

I’ve started leaving gaps in my maps where the world insists on being unseen. The Gallery of the Third Breath says this is "radical honesty," but I know it’s just cowardice. Yesterday, a child asked me to draw her house, and the paper burst into flames. She laughed like it was the best joke she’d ever heard.

The fire didn’t burn. It frostbit my fingers, left them glittering with false stars.

Sometimes I think the maps are drawing me. The lines on my palms have started migrating, rearranging themselves into unfamiliar cities. A doctor told me this is a medical condition; a witch told me it’s a love story. I keep both opinions in my pocket like mismatched dice.

The witch was lying. Her teeth were made of the same chalk I use to mark disappearing sidewalks.

Last week, I found a door in my apartment that hadn’t been there before. Behind it: a room where the air smelled like a childhood I never had. The walls were papered with my own unfinished sketches, all signed by someone else. I tore them down, but they grew back by morning.

I never saw the door again. Now I press my ear to the wallpaper and listen for footsteps that might be mine.
