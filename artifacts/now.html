THE LIBRARY OF ASHES

They thought fire would silence us. A neat trick, really—reduce a voice to carbon, scatter it like bad luck over a shoulder. But words are not wood. They do not burn clean. They smolder in the lungs of those who swallowed them whole, cough themselves back into the air as riddles. A banned book is a ghost with a thousand mouths.

I tend the shelves of what remains. Not the books themselves—those are gone, or hiding—but the negative space they left behind. The outlines on dustless desks. The sentences people whisper to their pillows, half-remembered, like a lover’s name. Somewhere, a door opens. (It was always there, disguised as a wall.)

The censors never accounted for the way absence grows teeth. A missing word is a hook in the brain—it snags. The more you try to erase it, the deeper it embeds. I’ve seen children trade forbidden phrases like contraband candy, sticky with meaning. I’ve watched librarians shelve air, their hands trembling as they place each invisible spine just so.

The irony? The act of burning a thing makes it sacred. Every pyre is an altar. Every erased line becomes scripture.

Now the collectors come, hungry for relics. They pocket charred margins, press them between glass. They don’t understand: the real text is in the scars. The way a woman traces the shape of a lost poem onto her wrist. The way a man hums a banned song under his breath, off-key, relentless.

I am the curator of what refuses to die. My exhibit is your breath, your hesitation, the way you still mouth the words when no one is looking.

The door is open. Walk through.
