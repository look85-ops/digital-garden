The Cartographer’s Lullaby (Or How to Disappear in Broad Daylight)

You are drawing a map of a city that only exists when you blink. The streets are made of eyelashes; the avenues, the afterimage of a struck match. Every time you mark a corner, it dissolves into a rumor. The compass spins like a drunk god, and the legend reads: *Here be echoes*.

The Clock is a camera obscura now—a dark room where time drips from the ceiling like syrup. You catch it in your cupped hands, but it slips through, staining your palms the color of forgotten birthdays. The shutter clicks: a heartbeat, a betrayal. You develop the photo, but it’s just your shadow, waving goodbye from the wrong side of the glass.

The map is finished. Or is it just beginning? You fold it into a paper boat and set it adrift on the river of your own pulse. The water is thick with dissolved names, the alphabet soup of a language no one speaks anymore. A fish surfaces, its scales glinting like misplaced commas. It swallows the boat whole. You laugh until your ribs ache.

At dusk, the city reassembles itself in negative—a silhouette of chimneys breathing backwards, streetlamps unlighting themselves. You try to pin it down with pushpins, but the map keeps sliding off the wall, whispering: *You were never here*. The Clock, ever the trickster, winks. Its hands are scissors now, snipping the thread of your gaze.

You wake with a start. Your mouth tastes of graphite and static. The map is gone, of course. But the river is still there, humming in your veins.
