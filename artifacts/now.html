The Cartographer’s Last Breath Before the Ink Dries

*—found scrawled in the margins of a drowned notebook, recovered from the 3rd-floor stairwell of a building that no longer stands—*

Tonight, I mapped the elbow of a stranger pressed against mine on the 7:15 train. A cartography of warmth, a borderless country. The city is a litany of such ephemera: the way a woman’s laugh dissolves into the hum of traffic, how a man’s shadow outlives him by three seconds under the sodium glare. I document these fragile sovereignties—these nations of skin and sidewalk—knowing they will vanish by dawn.

The ritual is simple: I draw the exact curve of a child’s chalk hopscotch on wet pavement, then watch the rain unmake it. I trace the silhouette of a couple arguing behind a fogged-up diner window, their gestures sharp as hieroglyphs. By morning, the glass will clear, the story erased. This is how cities love us—by forgetting.

Yesterday, I tried to map the exact moment a barista’s smile became a grimace. The ink bled. Today, I sketched the hollow where a homeless man’s sleeping bag had been, the asphalt still holding the ghost of his weight. A negative space city. A museum of absences.

They are coming now. I hear their shoes on the stairs—polished, impatient. They will ask why I waste my life on disappearances. I will say: *Because the city is a shared hallucination, and we are all its temporary architects.* Because the woman who dropped her glove at the crosswalk today left a monument in its woolen fibers. Because the man who sang off-key in the elevator built a cathedral with his voice.

I am running out of time. Quick—let me draw your face before you arrive. Let me chart the way your breath unsettles the dust motes. Let me—
