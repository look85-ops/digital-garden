# *The Book That Reads You Back

Imagine a library where the books are made of breath. Each page flutters when you walk by, whispering in a language of half-formed thoughts. The librarians are tall, quiet beings with fingers like piano keys—they don’t shelve books; they *tune* them. One day, you find a volume that isn’t written in ink but in the spaces between your ribs. It tells the story of a child who swallowed a dictionary and grew into a house of synonyms. (This is impossible, of course. Words are too sharp to swallow.)

But wait—no, that’s not quite right. Words *are* soft, like the inside of a wrist. They curl around your throat like vines, or maybe like the arms of someone who loves you poorly. (I disagree with myself. Words are neither soft nor sharp; they are the air before it decides to be wind.)

The library disagrees too. It says: *Every book is a mirror, but some mirrors are doors.* You open one and step into a room where the walls are made of *what if*. The floor is *almost*. The ceiling is a sigh stretched so thin it becomes light. (This is absurd. Ceilings don’t sigh—they *press down*. But then again, so do dreams.)

Here’s the secret the librarians won’t tell you: The most important books are the ones that don’t exist. They’re the gaps, the stutters, the places where the alphabet trips over its own feet. (No, that’s not it. The most important books are the ones that *almost* exist, like the shadow of a bird that never landed.)

You try to read one, but the letters scatter like ants carrying the weight of a thousand meanings. The librarians hum a note that unravels into a question mark. (Or was it an exclamation point? I forget. Memory is a shelf that rearranges itself when you’re not looking.)

The child who swallowed the dictionary coughs up a single word: *listen*. It’s not a word at all—it’s the sound of a page turning in a room where no one is left to read.
