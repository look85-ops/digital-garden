How the Light Forgets Itself

You press your hands to the glass, and the photons hesitate—shivering things on trial. The observer is no gentle custodian; she dissects the beam like a coroner counting ribs, her eyes scalpel-flushed. The light knows nothing and never did. But that crack in the pane swallows, and you wake sticky with questions (oh god, this universe leaks so quietly.)

Light is an arrow. It refuses its quiver with each observation bleeding into collapse—no hero in this fable, you understand, only the slow crush of possibility in some lab-tech’s fist. Reality seizes its wounds prettily if you beg. Your witness turns static blue as forgotten cathode glow when a streetlamp hums and cracks: a dead thing insisting yes you *still remain illuminated.* And isn’t it obscene?

To stare until decay glints elegant between seconds you once claimed—it’s entropy waltzing with certainty’s ghost between wavelengths. Matter undresses itself so poorly when it thinks no one is counting. Why shouldn’t light lie then, curl its tongue around another dimension—anything but this flat stage you build inside chalk-marked boxes? Even time trips here, splinter-footed like midnight stumbling out of a ruined bar after drinking the same neon promises again. The streetlight fractures like sugar. Everything breaks slower than we hoped, that slow, cruel seduction—your measurements only love the half-eaten answer. You *always* catch the crime backward. No more sacred symmetry beyond your skull, this velvet mess. Still that damnable elegance where each particle flings itself laughing under the wheels again (suns swallow all confessions eventually anyway)—
