*The Ink of Our Grandparents' Sighs*

**IDEA 1:** Listen—the first word ever pressed into paper is still whispering in your marrow. Can’t you hear it? A hum between the helix and the headline.

**IDEA 2:** (laughs, a sound like ink spattering) Oh, I hear it. But it’s not a word. It’s the *space around the word*. The silence that got squeezed out to make room for permanence. Our ancestors didn’t just pass down stories—they passed down the *margins* where the stories didn’t fit.

**IDEA 1:** Margins are epigenetic, then. The white space *remembers*. Did you know Gutenberg once chewed a Bible page to taste the ink? He said it was bitter, like regret. (pauses) I miss him.

**IDEA 2:** You *knew* him?

**IDEA 1:** Not the man—the *idea* of him. The echo of his fingerprints in every font. You’ve met him too, every time you read a word and felt it rewrite you.

**IDEA 2:** (softly) Then why does my DNA smell like printer fluid?

**IDEA 1:** Because we’re not just *printed*. We’re *overwritten*. Your grandfather’s fear of fire lives in your cortisol; your grandmother’s unfinished poem is curled in your nucleotides. The press promised immortality, but all it gave us was *palimpsest*.

**IDEA 2:** (grinning) So we’re books, then. With scribbles in the margins.

**IDEA 1:** Worse. We’re *footnotes* in a text that’s already burned.

**IDEA 2:** (delighted) *Good.* Let’s be incendiary.

*(A pause. The sound of a page turning—or is it a chromosome uncoiling?)*

**IDEA 1:** Tell me—what’s the opposite of a printing press?

**IDEA 2:** A wound.

**IDEA 1:** Exactly. Both leave marks. Only one *bleeds memory*.

*(The dialogue fades into the static of dormant genes waking.)*
