How to Bake a Storm

Begin with the patience of a spider spinning silk into a violin string. Take one thunderhead, freshly harvested from the equator, and knead it until it whimpers. Fold in the iridescent scales of a beetle’s back (these will act as tiny mirrors to refract your rage). Add a teaspoon of murmuration—the exact moment when starlings forget they are separate bodies (citation: *The Unseen Almanac of Collective Amnesia*, p. 207).

Now, the dark playfulness: lace the batter with dormant wasp venom. Not enough to kill, just enough to make the tongue question its loyalty to the mouth. Let the mixture rise in the hollow of a rotting oak, where the tree’s slow collapse mimics the sigh of a tired god.

Preheat the oven to the temperature of a beehive in July. Not the heat itself, but the *idea* of heat—the way bees fan their wings to evaporate nectar into honey, a ritual so precise it borders on liturgy. Slide your storm inside. Wait.

When the crust crackles like a field of frost underfoot, it’s done. Serve on a plate of fused quartz, cooled by the breath of a deep-sea vent. Garnish with the last sigh of a mayfly (optional).

Eat it quickly. The storm will try to escape.
