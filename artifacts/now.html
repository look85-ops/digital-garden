The Diary of the Girl Who Swallowed the Moon

*—found sewn into the belly of a moth, ink like old blood, pages soft as skin—*

**Entry the Seventh, or the First, or the Never:**

Tonight, I ate the moon again. It’s a habit, I confess. Not the whole thing, just a sliver—the kind that hooks into your gums like a fishbone. It tastes of salt and static, the way a scream might if you could lick it. Mother says I’ll regret it when my teeth turn to glass, but I’ve already spit out three molars and they clinked like coins in a wishing well. I call that luck.

I keep the shards in a matchbox under my tongue. When the doctors come (they arrive every Thursday, wearing gloves made of spider silk), I let them peek inside. They shudder and scribble notes in a language that looks like ants marching. One of them told me I was “a tragedy in reverse.” I think he meant it as a compliment.

At midnight, the moon regrows. It’s shy about it, blushing silver as it stitches itself back together. I watch from the windowsill, legs swinging, toes curling like question marks. Sometimes, I whisper to it: *You’re mine now. You’re mine now.* The moon never answers, but its silence is a kind of complicity.

**Midpoint Interruption:**

Then the taxidermist arrived.

He wasn’t invited. He never is. He waltzed in wearing a waistcoat stitched from crow feathers and demanded I pay him in “lunar interest.” I laughed until my ribs ached. He didn’t find it funny. He never does. Instead, he pulled a needle from his pocket—long as a lie, sharp as a secret—and began stitching the shadows to the floor. “This,” he said, “is how you keep a thing from running away.”

I bit his finger. It tasted of formaldehyde and forgotten birthdays. He left in a huff, but not before warning me that “the moon always collects its debts.”

I don’t believe him. The moon is too busy being swallowed.

**Postscript:**

The doctors say I’m “aestheticizing my own unraveling.” I say they’re just jealous because their bones don’t glow in the dark.
