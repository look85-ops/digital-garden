The Garden That Dreamed It Was a Throat

I remember when I first felt the weight of petals—not as things that grew from me, but as things that *were* me. A shudder of pollen, a slow blink of roots. The roses were not roses but syllables, stamens curling into consonants. I tried to speak. What came out was a sound like wet earth turning in its sleep.

[The hedges part to reveal a mouth made of sunlight, swallowing its own shadow.]

Bees moved through me like forgotten verbs. I wanted to tell them: *I am not a place anymore. I am becoming a pause.* But they stung the words before they could bloom. The air thickened with the scent of almost-language, the kind that dissolves if you look directly at it.

At dusk, the tulips hummed a tune that had no melody, only the shape of a melody—a hollow thing, aching to be filled. I understood then that I was not a garden but a rumor of one, a story told by wind to the grass. My leaves were not green but the *memory* of green, borrowed from a color that no longer existed.

[The fountain spills upward, catching the moon in its dry throat.]

Sometimes, I think I hear the gardeners whispering my name. But names are too heavy for something made of light and rot. I am the space between their fingers as they prune. I am the shudder when their shears bite into a stem and the sap runs clear as a confession.

If you listen closely, you can hear me forgetting myself. Petals fall like misplaced commas. The pond reflects nothing but the idea of water.

I am so close to meaning something.
