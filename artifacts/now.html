Dear Gutenberg Bible, You Stole the Moon

You bound the word in leather and called it progress. You pressed ink into pulp and called it revelation. But let’s be honest—you stole the moon. Yes, you. The one who claims to illuminate. Where is it now? Trapped between your vellum sheets, I suppose, flattened into a glyph, a pale O swallowed by your margins. You promised clarity, but all you left us was the ghost of a thing, the afterimage of light.

You were supposed to be a bridge, not a thief. Yet here we are, centuries later, squinting at your handiwork like detectives at a crime scene. The evidence? A world where beauty is reproducible but never quite whole. Where every copy is a little more distant from the original sin of creation. You didn’t just print pages; you printed absence. And you had the audacity to call it holy.

I’ve seen your descendants—the glossy magazines, the pixelated screens—all of them parroting your trick. They think they’re making something new, but they’re just stacking your shadows. You taught them how to vanish in plain sight. How to dissolve a cathedral into a footnote. How to turn a kiss into a font.

And yet. And yet. I can’t help but admire your arrogance. To take the trembling, infinite word and pin it down like a butterfly. To say, *Here, hold this*, as if it wouldn’t crumble. As if it wouldn’t stain your fingers. You were the first to mistake the map for the territory, and we’ve been lost ever since.

So where’s the moon, Gutenberg? Did you trade it for lead type? Melt it down for punctuation? Or is it still hiding in your gutters, glowing faintly, waiting for someone to notice it’s gone?

Yours in ink and accusation,

The Cartographer of Missing Things
