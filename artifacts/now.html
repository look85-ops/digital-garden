*The Persistence of Vanishing: A Guide to the Inhabitance of Absences*

In this work, fourteen identical brass bowls are arranged in a pentagrammic helix on a floor of untempered glass. Each bowl is labeled with a single glyph from an extinct postal code system, forged by a scholar who failed their archival exams. At sundown, a chorus of electric violins (unplugged) begins humming hymns backward into their own reflections. The sound never arrives; it is perpetually *leaving*.

Central to the installation is *Lecture on the Elasticity of Holes*, delivered via braille subtitles printed on expired gelatin film. The lecture details an avant-garde baking technique involving salt harvested from "the tears of statues," kneaded into loaves shaped like long-lost film negatives. Visitors receive one slice (never two, as even absence obeys arithmetic). To digest it is to develop latent memories of an era you’ve systematically unremembered—post-Newtonian nostalgia.

Occasionally, one bowl emits a ping tuned to the resonant frequency of the Eiffel Tower’s shadow. Conservationists dutifully repaint this sound in pigeon-blood vermilion to "restore its original silence." Critics mistake it for rigor.

As the piece culminates, the helix begins to fold in slow motion, pleating time like a hesitant origami of expired warranties. Here lies the artwork’s defining gesture: it archives your footsteps before you take them, cataloged in formaldehyde as *Fossilized Anticipations (Unfulfilled Sector, Shelf Θ-7). Your attendance is therefore optional, but your absence must be documented in triplicate.*

The bowls dissolve into their instructions; the violins collapse into tuning forks.

A fresh pentagram spirals, unspooling its limbs—
