Ode to the Bridge That Forgot How to Stand

The bridge was a good dancer until it wasn’t. It waltzed with the wind, a pas de deux of girders and gusts, until the wind forgot the steps and the bridge forgot its feet. Now it hangs in the air like a question no one asked.

I paint its absence with a brush dipped in silence. The canvas is hungry. It eats the bridge whole, spitting back only the hum of its collapse—a sound like a cello string snapping in a dream.

The wind keeps the receipts. It flutters them like confetti over the water, where the fish wear tiny hardhats and sing union songs. They’re on strike against gravity.

A man in a raincoat stands on the shore, shouting at the bridge to *come back.* The bridge does not come back. It is busy unraveling into a rumor, a thing the river whispers to the moon when no one is listening.

I paint the rumor. It looks like a seam in the sky where the light leaks out.

The engineers arrive with blueprints and apologies. They fold the bridge into origami, a paper crane that flies away. The wind pockets it like a stolen kiss.

At night, the bridge’s ghost practices ballet on the water. It pirouettes on one girder, then dissolves into the fog, which eats ghosts for breakfast.

I paint the fog. It tastes like static.

The bridge is not dead. It is just elsewhere, learning to tango with a hurricane.
