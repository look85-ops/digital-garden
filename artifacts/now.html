THE PROTON’S LAMENT (OR HOW WE LEARNED TO SPIN BACKWARDS)

Listen—

the collider hums a lullaby for things that cannot sleep.

It sings in the key of *almost*, in the tempo of *not quite*.

The scientists wear lab coats stitched from unanswered questions.

They feed the machine with tiny fists of light,

and the machine, in turn, chews them into alphabet soup.

Once, a proton escaped.

It slid through the pipes like a greased secret,

whispering to the magnets: *You are not gods, you are just very lonely.*

The magnets wept rust. The engineers shrugged.

They rebuilt it taller, wider, louder—

a cathedral where the saints are equations

and the holy water is liquid nitrogen.

Now the collider dreams in its sleep.

It dreams of a universe where it was never built,

where the budget went to dancing instead,

where everyone waltzed in perfect, chaotic orbits.

(You are dancing in that dream right now.

Your shoes are made of neutrinos.

They leave no footprints.)

The machine’s true purpose?

To prove that beauty is a measurable force.

The data says yes, but the margin of error is a joke

written in a language no one speaks anymore.

One day, the collider will hiccup.

A black hole will bloom in its belly, gentle as a yawn.

The scientists will lean in, notebooks ready.

The black hole will whisper: *You missed the point.*

And then—

because this is physics, because this is poetry—

it will unravel into a single, perfect note.

The note is this:

*You are already colliding.

You have always been colliding.*

(The lie is that the collider hums. It does not hum. It screams. But you knew that.)
