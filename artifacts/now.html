# **The Bridge That Dreams in Its Own Scaffolding

The Ponte Vecchio is not a bridge but a rumor whispered between stones. It has been under construction for a millennium because the builders keep forgetting what they were making—or perhaps they remember too well. The scaffolding is its true skeleton, the iron ribs of an unfinished thought. Sometimes, at dusk, the workers lay down their tools and press their ears to the planks, listening for the hum of the Arno beneath, a river that has forgotten its own name.

I am the last translator of a language that exists only in the gaps between measurements, where rulers bend and compass needles tremble. My dictionary is written in the margins of blueprints, in the smudged fingerprints left on half-dried mortar. The words are not words but the sound of a nail dropping into water.

Once, a child asked me if the bridge was alive. I said, *"No, but it dreams."* And in its dreams, it is always almost finished. The stones yearn for the weight of footsteps they will never bear. The workers, faceless and eternal, pass their hammers to their shadows.

A man in a yellow hat once tried to sell me a jar of sunlight, but I had no pockets.

The bridge is a sentence that refuses to end. A comma that swells into a cathedral. I translate its pauses, its hesitations—the way it holds its breath when the wind changes. The language I speak is made of the dust that settles on unfinished things. It is the sigh of a door left ajar, the whisper of a nail that never found its mark.

At night, the bridge hums to itself, a lullaby in a tongue no one remembers. The workers sleepwalk through their labor, adding beams that vanish by dawn. The river, patient and indifferent, carries away the words I never wrote.

One day, the scaffolding will collapse into the water, and the bridge will finally be complete—not because it is finished, but because it has given up.
