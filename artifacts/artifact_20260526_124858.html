«THE GOVERNMENT HAS NOT YET ISSUED A STATEMENT»

A gallery space, dimly lit, suffocated by the weight of unsaid things. Twelve typewriters, each suspended from the ceiling by thin wires, hover just above twelve identical oak desks. None of them have paper. Their keys click ceaselessly, violently, like the teeth of someone trying not to scream. The sound is rhythmic but uneven—a bureaucrat’s heartbeat.

On the far wall: a projection of a blank sheet of paper, slowly burning at the edges. The flames never reach the center.

Visitors are given small wooden blocks engraved with words that do not exist in any language. They are instructed to place them in a clear plexiglass box at the center of the room. The box is already full. No one knows who filled it.

We apologize—no, actually, we don’t. The apology was a test. Did you flinch? Did you crave the resolution of politeness? The piece is functioning as intended.

A recording plays on loop: a voice whispering the same sentence in an extinct dialect of an Eastern European language that was never written down. The translation, provided on a small plaque, reads: *"Silence is not an absence. It is a room where the walls are made of your own listening."*

Occasionally, one of the typewriters stops. A gallery attendant in a frayed Soviet-era suit approaches it, gently lifts the machine, and replaces it with another. The broken one is carried into a back room. If you press your ear to the door, you can hear it being dismantled.

At random intervals, the lights flicker off. When they return, the desks have shifted imperceptibly. Or have they? The visitors exchange glances. No one speaks.

The plexiglass box is now overflowing. The blocks spill onto the floor. A child picks one up. The word engraved on it is illegible—or maybe it was never there to begin with.

We regret nothing.

(Do you see it now? The piece was never about the typewriters. Or the burning paper. Or the box. It was about the moment you realized you were holding your breath.)
